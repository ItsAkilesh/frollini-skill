# Writing questions for interviews and focus groups

Sources: the CMU 17-603 lecture on interviewing, based on Krueger and Casey, *Focus Groups: A Practical Guide for Applied Research* (4th ed.). Paraphrased.

This covers gathering information from users, customers, or colleagues, as in requirements interviews, customer discovery, and post-mortems. It does not cover job interviews.

## An interview works only if

- the person understands the question
- the setting makes an honest answer feel safe
- they actually know the answer
- they can put it into words
- you can understand what they say

## Good questions

- Sound like conversation and use the respondent's own words.
- Are short and easy to say aloud.
- Have only one possible meaning.
- Are usually open-ended.
- Ask about one thing at a time.

## Sequence

| Stage | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Opening | An easy, factual question that gets everyone talking |
| Introductory | Connects people to the topic |
| Transition | Moves toward the core, often by asking people to "think back" |
| Key | The two to five questions that matter; most of the time goes here |
| Ending | Reflection, ranking, and "Is there anything we didn't ask?" |

Within that sequence, go from general to specific, positive to negative (with time for both), and uncued to cued. Ask openly first, then raise topics people didn't mention to see whether the gap matters.

## Phrasing

- Prefer "What do you like best about the current process?" to "How satisfied are you with...?" The second is a closed question in disguise.
- "Think back to the first time you used it. What happened?" draws out real experience. Use it only for recent or memorable events.
- Avoid "why." It sounds like an interrogation and gets rationalized answers. Ask "What led you to...?"
- Hold back examples. They narrow people's thinking, so save them for follow-up probes.
- Activities make questions engaging: listing, rating, choosing between options, sketching a flow, or imagining a scenario.

## Building the question set

1. Brainstorm with people who understand the goal, keeping one note-taker. Start from "What do we need to know by the end?"
2. Phrase the questions using the rules above.
3. Sequence them.
4. Time each question. Too many questions means shallow answers.
5. Get feedback. Will these get us what we need? Are the words right? Is anything redundant or missing?
6. Revise, without endless nitpicking.
7. Test on someone like the real respondents and watch for hesitation or confusion.

Afterward, consolidate the notes, share the findings with the people who need them, and turn them into actions.

## Angry or skeptical groups

Acknowledge the frustration once, then set ground rules. Don't apologize, defend, or explain the roadmap; you are there to listen. Give everyone a turn so one loud voice doesn't take over. Steer venting toward specifics: "Walk me through the moment it froze." Finish with prioritization so the anger turns into a ranked list of problems.
