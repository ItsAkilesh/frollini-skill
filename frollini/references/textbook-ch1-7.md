# Barrett, *Leadership Communication*: practical notes, chapters 1–7

Paraphrased notes on the textbook. Page numbers refer to the printed book.

## Chapter 1: What is leadership communication?

**Core idea.** Leadership is exercised largely through communication: managers spend most of their working day communicating. The book's target is the *transformational* leader, who earns trust, connects with people, states a clear and motivating vision, and moves others to act (p. 4).

**The rhetorical situation (Exhibit 1.1, p. 6).** Every message involves a communicator, a message, a medium, and an audience, all inside a context. Breakdowns happen when any of these interferes: noise in the context, the wrong medium, poorly chosen words, or a weak image of the sender. The medium shapes how the message is received, so choose it on purpose.

**Working definition (p. 7).** Leadership communication is the deliberate, purposeful transfer of meaning that influences a person, group, organization, or community. It anticipates interference, reads the context and the audience, picks the right medium, and builds messages that guide, direct, motivate, or inspire action.

**Framework (Exhibit 1.2, p. 8).** Strategy sits at the center. Around it are the core skills: writing, speaking, emotional intelligence and interpersonal skills, and intercultural communication. These then extend outward to teams, meetings, organizational communication, and internal and external relations. Strategy applies at every level.

**Ethos is your main persuasive asset (pp. 9–16).**
- Aristotle's three appeals: *logos* (logic), *pathos* (emotion), *ethos* (the perceived character of the sender). If the audience does not trust the sender, logic and emotion carry little weight.
- Credibility is the top reason people follow a leader. To be credible you must come across as knowledgeable, authoritative, confident, honest, and trustworthy.
- Most managers think they are more credible than they are. Check how you come across through honest self-reflection and 360-degree feedback.
- Practical levers: reputation (title, track record), professional appearance suited to the culture, confidence (eye contact, rapport, speaking without notes), and precise language.

**Exhibit 1.4: ingredients of a positive ethos (p. 16).**
| Outward manifestation | Inner character | Communication actions |
|---|---|---|
| Appropriate dress and behavior; charisma; preparation and knowledge; documented expertise (education, credentials, experience) | Honest, honorable, truthful, fair, ethical | Skilled speaking and writing; empathetic listening; appropriate nonverbals; sincere connection; emotional intelligence; sensitivity to cultural differences |

**Sources of power (Exhibit 1.3, p. 13).** Coercive (threat of punishment), reward (expected benefits), legitimate (position or title), referent (personal appeal or charisma), expert (expertise), plus information (holding needed information) and connection (relationships). When several of these line up, the audience is more open to persuasion.

**Five traits of trusted people (p. 14):** integrity, competence, consistency, loyalty (willingness to protect and support others), and openness (sharing ideas and information freely). Spin that drifts away from what you actually believe damages trust.

**Ethical decision tools (pp. 17–20).**
- The golden rule appears across all major religions.
- Four traditional approaches: end results (consequentialist: the greatest good for the greatest number), duty (deontological: rights, rules, principles), social contract (the norms of the community), and personal virtue (conscience).
- **Hosmer's analytical process (Exhibit 1.6):** (1) understand the moral standards involved and the impacts: who benefits, who is harmed, which rights are exercised and which are denied; (2) define the full moral problem; (3) weigh economic outcomes, legal requirements, and ethical duties; (4) propose a convincing moral solution.
- Quick check: the "front-page" or Internet test. Would you be comfortable seeing this decision published for everyone to read?

**Self-improvement plan (Application 1.1).** Rate yourself from 1 to 5 in each area (ethos, audience analysis and strategy, written communication, oral, visual, emotional intelligence for self and others, cultural, team, organizational, internal, external). Then set goals, action steps, deadlines, and a way to measure success for each.

## Chapter 2: Leadership communication purpose, strategy, and structure

**Strategy has two parts (p. 28):** (1) decide what you want to achieve, meaning your purpose; (2) decide how best to achieve it with this audience. People skip this step because they are short of time. That is a main cause of careless workplace writing.

### Establish a clear purpose (pp. 29–32)
Professional communication usually has one of four overarching goals:
- **Inform:** pass on facts or data.
- **Influence or persuade:** get someone to believe, feel, or act.
- **Instruct:** teach a process, procedure, or policy.
- **Engage:** start a collaborative exchange, as on social channels.

Before drafting, ask: What should they *know* afterward? What should they *do*? What is my intention? A purpose can start simple ("inform") and turn out to be harder ("persuade" when the decision breaks from current strategy). Then you need a rationale and the pros and cons ready before you write. Clarifying the purpose is iterative, so revisit it after a first draft. If you set up an ongoing channel (blog, feed), state its purpose to followers and tell them if it changes.

**Clear thinking leads to clear writing (Swift memo example, pp. 30–32).** A muddled draft usually means muddled thinking. Emotion clouds judgment, so an angry first draft comes out accusatory, distant ("people employed by this company"), and threatening. The rewrite uses "we," takes a positive and solution-focused tone, and is shorter and clearer.

**Subject-line test.** A subject line, or the title of a report or talk, should capture the purpose and set the tone (neutral "Use of Copiers," not "Abuse of Copiers"). If you cannot write one that states your intent, your purpose is not yet clear to you, and it will not be clear to the reader.

### Consider the context: questions to ask (p. 33)
1. What else is happening (world, industry, organization, the reader's own life) that will affect how this is received?
2. Where does this message sit in the overall flow? Is it first or last?
3. What came before it and what comes after?
4. What are the organizational implications?
5. What are the implications for people?
6. What does the audience know or believe about the context, compared with what you know?
7. What does the audience expect from this medium?
8. What cultural differences matter?

Example of failure: a company announced layoffs while sponsoring a high-profile golf tournament, which damaged its reputation for years. In uncertain times, word cost-related messages so they reassure.

### Communication strategy framework (Exhibit 2.1 and key questions, Exhibit 2.2, pp. 34–36)
Components: **communicator, purpose, messages, media/forum, timing, feedback, all resting on the audience.**

| Component | Questions to ask yourself |
|---|---|
| Purpose | What do I want to accomplish? Do I have related purposes for different audiences? How does this support the organization's strategy? |
| Message | What is my overall message? How do I make it acceptable to this audience? What do I expect them to do? What are the supporting messages, and do they differ by audience? |
| Medium/forum | Which channel (e-mail, blog, memo, letter, meeting, speech, podcast) best reaches each audience? What about cost and logistics? |
| Audiences | Who are the primary, secondary, and accidental audiences? What are their interests and stakes? How will the message affect them? How should I ask for their feedback? |
| Communicator | Am I the best person to deliver this? If not, who would be most credible for each audience? |
| Timing | Does timing matter? When, and in what order, should each message go out? |
| Feedback | How will I know the message was received as intended? How will I measure its impact? |

Use the full framework for important or formal communication. For quick messages, at least pause to consider the audience, the message, and the medium. Keep the overarching message consistent across audiences, and tailor the emphasis and supporting messages to each. (Investors may welcome a merger that frightens employees.) Expect employees to act as informal spokespeople on social media, and plan for that.

**Exhibit 2.3 (merger strategy matrix, p. 37).** Lay out the strategy as a table with one row per audience (investment community, local media, national media, acquirer's employees, acquired company's employees) and columns for medium, purpose, message, communicator, and timing. A real merger plan of this kind covered more than 25 audiences, with timing down to the minute.

### Analyze the audience
**Exhibit 2.4: questions to clarify the audience (p. 38).**
1. Is this a reply to an earlier message or request? Where does it sit in the overall flow?
2. Who are the primary audiences (the people who will act), the secondary audiences, and any accidental ones?
3. If they make the decision, what motivates them, and how are they best persuaded?
4. If they do not decide, how are they related to the person who does?
5. What do they already know, and what do they need to know?
6. What do I expect them to do?
7. How do I expect them to feel?
8. What do they gain or lose if they accept my recommendation?
9. How will this affect the organization?
10. Overall, what do I expect to happen as a result?

**By expertise (Exhibit 2.5, p. 39).**
| Audience | Typical media | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Layperson | Articles, newsletters, blogs, Twitter | Make it interesting and practical; give background; define terms; use stories and analogies |
| **Executive** | E-mail, memo, presentation, report | **Focus on the decision; keep it simple; be honest and direct; put conclusions and recommendations early** |
| Expert | Journal articles, reports, white papers, professional networks | State the how and why; keep background short; use the field's language; state inferences and conclusions; cite references |
| Technical | Reports, procedures | State the how and why; keep background short; stay practical and avoid theory |
| Combined (diverse) | E-mail, memo, report, blog | Avoid jargon; use sections and headings; define terms; move technical detail to appendixes; keep the prose clean and simple |

As you rise in an organization, your audiences become broader and less technical, so move from the expert approach toward the combined or nonexpert approach. Communities of practice (groups that share a knowledge base) can be addressed in their shared language.

**By decision-making style (Exhibit 2.6, p. 40; Williams and Miller).** More than half of sales presentations do not match the decision maker's style.
| Style | How they decide | How to communicate |
|---|---|---|
| Charismatics | Enthusiastic and drawn to new ideas, but decide on information rather than emotion | Lead with the bottom line; focus on results; give straightforward arguments; use visuals |
| Thinkers | Logical and data-driven; risk-averse; need time | Raise concerns up front; have plenty of data ready; show all perspectives; be ready to walk through methods; tolerate silence |
| Skeptics | Suspicious and combative; need someone they trust | Borrow credibility from someone they trust; stress the credibility of your sources; use concrete examples |
| Followers | Cautious; rely on track records | Give proven methods, references, testimonials, and case studies; show that the idea is safe yet new; offer options and details |
| Controllers | Analytical; dislike ambiguity | Structure the argument carefully; bring expert detail; do not push; give the facts and let them decide |

Take the **"you attitude"**: what does this reader need to hear in order to agree and decide?

**By medium.** Each channel carries its own expectations. A CEO's blog post invites replies where a distribution-list e-mail does not. A Twitter account implies you will answer.

**By organizational context.** Think through reporting relationships and what motivates people. At minimum, for any important message you should be able to state your purpose and what you expect the reader to do.

### Organize the communication (pp. 41–46)
Structure depends on purpose, message, audience, and medium:
- With a hostile audience, consider an indirect structure that saves the recommendation for the end.
- With a detail-driven decision maker, present the facts first.
- In relationship-oriented cultures, open with general, non-business talk.
- **With busy executives, state the main point at once.**

Three planning questions: What is the best opening for this audience? How should I arrange the material so it is easy to follow and well received? What is the best way to close?

**General rules for busy readers:** get straight to the main message, stay on topic, show why it matters to them, and organize it around their decision. **Put the purpose first by default.** Include background only when it is needed for context. Put recommendations at the beginning unless you have a specific reason to delay them, because readers will hunt for them anyway. Make the logic of the whole piece and of each section obvious.

**Organizational devices (p. 43):** deduction (conclusion first, then support), induction (facts that build to a "therefore"), chronological, cause and effect, comparison and contrast, problem and solution, spatial. Most business writing is deductive or inductive.

**The Pyramid Principle (Minto; Exhibit 2.7, pp. 44–45).** Put one governing statement (the conclusion or recommendation) at the top. Each level below answers "why?", "what?", or "how?" for the level above. Rules:
1. **MECE**, meaning mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive: no overlap between ideas at the same level, and support that is balanced and complete enough.
2. Each level summarizes the ideas grouped beneath it.
3. Ideas in a group must be of the same kind.
4. Ideas in a group must be in a logical order.

Test: read the top level and the next level as one sentence ("X should do Y because A, B, and C"). It should flow and be parallel. A drawn pyramid exposes gaps, imbalance, and duplicated support, and it makes it easy to split work across a team.

**Storyboards (Exhibit 2.8, p. 46).** Sketch the deck as "ghost" slides: a title on each slide stating its point, with placeholder content. This shows the logical flow, suggests which chart each point needs, and lets a team split the slides among members. Typical deck flow: title, agenda, recommendation, supporting sections (market analysis, profitability, implementation), next steps.

## Chapter 3: The language of leaders

**Core idea.** Word choice and sentence construction make up your style. Readers experience that style as tone, and from tone they infer your ethos. Aim to sound confident and authoritative while staying approachable where the channel calls for it.

### Tone (pp. 57–63)
- **Bad-news messages:** a cold subject line ("Layoff"), a blunt opening, distancing passive voice ("your job will be abolished"), and a perfunctory thank-you all read as uncaring. The rewrite uses an indirect structure for bad news: context first, then the decision, then specifics such as severance, then sincere appreciation, delivered as a letter rather than an e-mail. A blunt active voice is not always better, for example when the writer did not personally make the decision.
- **Avoid words that undercut the decision or the reader.** Calling a selection process "arbitrary" makes the organization look foolish. Words like "naturally" or "you can see" can imply bad faith on the reader's part and cost them face.
- **Fielden's four styles (Exhibit 3.4):** stilted and formal, passive and impersonal, plain and personal, and breezy and casual. Most business readers prefer the plain, personal, direct style (a clear refusal, a brief reason, a sincere offer to help). Know your natural style and adjust it to the situation without straying so far that you sound insincere.
- Channels distort tone. E-mail can feel impersonal, and texts can sound harsher than intended.
- Check your tone: read it aloud, rehearse or record talks, and ask someone else to read sensitive pieces. Ask yourself: How do I come across? Do I sound confident? Would people want to read this?

**Exhibit 3.5: guidelines for a positive ethos in writing and speaking (p. 64).**
1. Learn from good models, but develop your own voice.
2. Read your draft aloud. Get a second opinion on anything sensitive.
3. Never send anything while angry, especially by e-mail, text, or tweet.
4. Avoid "thesaurusitis," meaning big words where simple ones work.
5. Avoid text abbreviations in professional messages, since their meanings vary across people and cultures.
6. Use strong verbs. Avoid the passive voice unless you have a reason for it.
7. Prefer positive constructions to negative ones.
8. Cut empty modifiers ("sort of," "kind of," "possibly," "thing," and too many "-ly" words).
9. Use hedges sparingly ("I think," "I believe," "I feel," "in my opinion," "probably").
10. Cut spoken fillers ("like," "you know," "um").
11. Avoid "up speak," where statements end sounding like questions.
12. Use jargon only when it is the best word and you are sure the audience knows it.

These are preferences, not rules. Break one when you have a deliberate reason, such as rhythm, tact, or purposeful vagueness.

### Concise, but not too concise (pp. 65–66)
Concise writing tends to be clearer and holds attention. Over-compression through text-speak and obscure abbreviations causes misreading. Use standard spelling with anyone you do not know well or who is senior to you. Make sure neither verbosity nor terseness comes from haste.

### Directness: nine techniques (pp. 66–70)
1. **Active voice by default.** Put the actor first. Passive is acceptable when you want to protect the actor, when the actor is unknown or unimportant, when a house or discipline style requires it, or for variety. Use "I" and "we" rather than contorting sentences to avoid them; inclusive "we" builds connection. Avoid the stilted "one" in US business writing. Grammar checkers often mislabel past-tense sentences as passive.
2. **Limit expletive openers** ("there is," "it is … that"), unless the alternative reads worse.
3. **Replace wordy prepositional idioms** (table, pp. 67–68): due to the fact that → because; during the time that → while; for the purpose of / in order to / with the purpose of → to; in the event that / if the conditions are such that → if; in the area of / in the case of → in; in the interest of → for; in the region of → around; with regard to → regarding; over and above → beyond. Keep one only when it adds clarity, for example for text that will be translated.
4. **Cut needless relative pronouns** ("who," "which," "that"): "Mr. B. is a man who never misses a meeting" becomes "Mr. B. never misses a meeting."
5. **Avoid needless repetition.** In lists, move repeated words into the lead-in sentence and start each item with an active verb. Repeat only on purpose, for emphasis or rhetorical effect.
6. **Avoid vague, noncommittal words**, for example: almost, aspect, basis, elements, field, situation, things, virtually, up to, manner, help, feel, special.
7. **Watch jargon.** It is fine inside your community of practice, but it excludes or confuses broader, cross-functional, cross-cultural, and senior audiences. The higher you rise, the less of it you should use.
8. **Use active verbs, not nominalizations:** "perform an investigation … for a determination" becomes "investigate … to determine." If a word can be cut, cut it.
9. **Use the simple word over the complex one.** Simplify.

Wordiness, inflated language, and inappropriate abbreviations can read as evasion, carelessness, or indifference to the reader.

### Making messages memorable and persuasive (pp. 71–73)
1. **Tell a story,** about yourself or someone else. Stories humanize the speaker, keep complex material engaging, and stick in memory.
2. **Repeat strategically.** Repeating a key word, phrase, or clause, especially when speaking and especially near the close, builds emphasis and an emotional rallying point.
3. **Use parallel structure.** Make items in a series, list, or bullet set match grammatically. It eases reading, adds rhythm, and aids recall. Check every bulleted list and slide for parallelism. Example of the fix: "sign up …, complete forms …, pay by credit card, and pin numbers …" rather than mixing in "payment should be …"

### Social media style (p. 73)
1. Be honest and enter the space humbly. Prove your expertise rather than claiming it.
2. Say something meaningful. Listen first and add to the conversation.
3. Do not over-compress or misuse abbreviations, and avoid "me too" posts.

### Language rules that matter (pp. 74–76)
Errors damage your credibility and your organization's. Some employers screen candidates with grammar tests. Employers expect writing to be accurate, clear, and correct. Follow the conventions of your discipline and country. Examples: use the serial (Oxford) comma in legal and technical writing to avoid ambiguity, and in the US place periods and commas inside quotation marks, while many other countries do not.

**The most distracting error types:**
1. Sentence construction: fragments, run-ons, faulty parallelism, dangling modifiers, subject-verb agreement.
2. Punctuation: apostrophes, missing or extra commas, overused dashes and ellipses.
3. Pronouns: disagreement with the antecedent, subject and object case ("between he and I").
4. Spelling and wrong words: it's/its, there/their.

References: *The Gregg Reference Manual*; *The Business Writer's Handbook*.

### Editing and proofreading (pp. 76–78)
Editing checks clarity, concision, and word choice. Proofreading comes after editing and catches errors in usage, grammar, spelling, and formatting. Do neither while you are still generating ideas.
1. Do not proofread while composing.
2. Let time pass between writing and proofing.
3. Know your personal error patterns and look for them.
4. Watch the trouble spots: transposed letters, confused words, pronoun agreement, dangling modifiers, apostrophes, and inconsistency (especially across slides).
5. Run the spell-checker but do not rely on it. Treat grammar and style checkers with caution.
6. Proof important documents on paper. Draft short posts in an editor first.
7. Read backwards, from the end toward the start, to catch typos.
8. Reread even quick e-mails and texts. Get a fresh pair of eyes on important documents.

**Exhibit 3.6: checklist for reviewing your own language (p. 78).**
- *Style and tone:* the tone is suitably formal or informal and not offensive to this audience; no inappropriate jargon or abbreviations; concise, without wordiness or passive constructions; no overused "-ly" modifiers or hedges; no poor word choices; no awkward or unclear constructions; clear, concise, and confident overall.
- *Usage and proofreading:* no grammar or usage errors, no punctuation errors, no spelling errors, no typos.

## Chapter 4: Using social media and creating other leadership correspondence

**Core idea.** Professional writing falls into two groups: correspondence (e-mail, texts, tweets, letters, memos) and reports. Writing is a "threshold skill" for being hired and promoted. A careless internal e-mail can reach the public and damage both a reputation and a stock price.

### Choosing the medium (pp. 86–88)
- Default to replying in the same medium you received. When you start the exchange, follow the organization's norms. When you are free to choose, pick the medium that fits the context and the message.
- **Exhibit 4.1, written media trade-offs:**
  - *Blogs:* accepted professionally and conversational, but must be kept current and responsive.
  - *E-mail:* fast and easy to send to many people, but it leaves a permanent record, can be discovered in litigation, can spread accidentally, and can lose its formatting.
  - *Facebook:* casual. It reaches fans but risks backlash if not monitored.
  - *LinkedIn:* a professional network aimed at targeted groups. Keep it current. Overuse reads as self-promotion.
  - *Text messages:* immediate, but too abbreviated for complex or sensitive topics and easy to send without thinking.
  - *Tweets:* immediate and conversational, with standards still forming.
  - *Letters:* formal, used for external or official matters, and noticeable, but slow.
  - *Memos:* informal and internal, and they let you control the layout. Both letters and memos leave a paper trail.

### Organizing correspondence (pp. 86–92)
Default pattern: **main message or bottom line first, then the support needed, then a courteous close.**

**Open indirectly only when:** the context is complex and part of an ongoing chain; the reader's culture expects pleasantries first; you need to cushion bad news; complicated logic has to be explained first; or you are raising a topic for discussion on social media.

**The CPF opening (p. 90).** Use it in e-mails, memos, letters, and also presentations (Ch. 6):
- **C, Context:** what prompted this message and what surrounds it, for example "In response to your e-mail of Oct 5 about …"
- **P, Purpose:** why you are writing, stated plainly, for example "I would like you to set up a team meeting for Monday at 10 a.m."
- **F, Foreshadowing:** what is coming and in what order, for example "three objectives: (1) progress, (2) issues, (3) next steps."
Then use the foreshadowed topics as headings, in the same order. Without the context, even a clear request can be ambiguous.

**The "so what?" test.** Cut any sentence, heading, or subject line the reader could answer with "so what?"

**Example structure (Exhibit 4.3, meeting-notes e-mail):** a subject line that states the content ("Meeting notes with next steps from Feb 1 meeting"); an opening with context, purpose, and the two topics; one bold heading per topic with bullets beneath; a "next steps" section; and a close that asks for corrections by a set date and gives contact details.

**Salutations.** Printed letters take "Dear [Name]." In professional e-mail, use at least the person's given name. Keep "Dear" when you use a title or surname, which signals seniority or unfamiliarity; leaving it out can seem rude. Texts can start directly.

**Closings.** Short pieces do not need a summary. End with a call to action, follow-up arrangements or contact information, anticipated problems, or a courtesy line. Professional e-mail takes a sign-off ("Sincerely," "Best," "Regards") and your name.

### Formatting correspondence (pp. 92–97)
- **Layout:** margins of at least 1 inch, aligned with the letterhead. Do not crowd the page or screen. Keep paragraphs short; one-sentence paragraphs are fine. Do not leave just one or two lines plus the signature on page 2. Avoid single-word "widow" lines.
- **Spacing and alignment:** single-spaced, a blank line between paragraphs, no first-line indent, left-aligned and ragged right. Do not fully justify, and override templates that do.
- **Fonts:** serif (Times New Roman, Garamond, Palatino) for longer printed text; sans serif for slides, the web, and short on-screen text. Use fonts that render on every system for mass e-mail. Use 11–12 pt. Avoid ALL CAPS (it reads as shouting) and underlining (it looks dated and clips descenders). Use bold or a larger size for emphasis, and keep to one font family.
- **Principles for headings (p. 95):**
  1. Short, meaningful, and consistent in style. Capture the "so what?"
  2. Grammatically parallel (all nouns, all "-ing" forms, all imperatives, and so on).
  3. Same font as the body, but bold, larger, or centered.
  4. Consistent capitalization, never all caps.
  5. No underlining.
- **Lists:** use numbers for more than five items. Keep items parallel and start each with the same part of speech, for example "Draft / Establish / Design / Publish," not a mix of verbs and nouns. Put a colon before a list only when the lead-in is a complete sentence. Use end periods on all items or on none.

### Social media (pp. 97–103)
**Professional blogs:**
1. Define the purpose (why the blog exists, whom it is for, what others in the space write) and give posts titles that state their purpose ("Upcoming Policy Changes," not "My Saturday").
2. Join the conversation and reply to readers.
3. Credit and link to sources.
4. Make it different. Do not use it only as a marketing channel. Mix the personal with the professional.
5. Keep writing on a schedule for posting, replying, and reading others.
Blogs can be more casual, but Ch. 3 rules still apply. Open with meaning, set the context, include only the details needed, and close gracefully.

**Twitter plan:** set fixed check-in times; decide which types of tweet serve your goals; schedule prepared tweets and add timely ones in between. For a business account: have a purpose beyond driving traffic; assign knowledgeable, trustworthy staff, not an intern alone; show real names or initials; follow quality accounts, because who you follow is public; use light, business-appropriate humor.

**LinkedIn:** keep the profile current, specific, and keyword-rich, since recruiters search it. Join groups. Invite only people you know, personalize each invitation, and use a current photo. Avoid obvious self-promotion. Remember that everyone, your employer included, can see your activity.

### E-mail (pp. 103–107)
- **Subject lines decide whether the message gets opened.** Write them like headlines that tell the reader what they get ("Agenda for Tomorrow's Meeting," not "FYI" or "Reminder"). Unless you have positional power, the subject line has to earn the open.
- **Tone is harsher in e-mail** than on paper. E-mail is permanent and can be forwarded anywhere. Cautionary example (Exhibit 4.5): a CEO's angry e-mail to managers, full of ALL CAPS and threats, leaked to the press, and the company's stock fell 22%. Never send in anger, and consider every possible audience before you hit send.

**Ten guidelines for effective e-mail (p. 107):**
1. Write a compelling subject line that captures the "so what?"
2. Format for easy reading: short paragraphs, no "so what?" content, fonts that work on every system, and no tables that may break.
3. Limit images, which spam filters may block or distort.
4. Include contact details and working links.
5. Put your reply at the top of the thread, not the bottom.
6. Delete clutter such as repeated signatures and needless confidentiality boilerplate.
7. Be careful with Reply All, but do copy everyone involved.
8. Change the subject line when the topic changes.
9. Avoid very informal writing (jargon, contractions, abbreviations, emoticons) with coworkers.
10. Be careful with content and tone. Assume it will go public (the front-page test). Avoid sounding angry, condescending, or critical.

**Text messages (p. 108).** In professional texts, use complete words and sentences, a greeting, and your name, and proofread before sending. "Sent from my phone" does not excuse errors.

**Legal (p. 109).** E-mail, texts, and tweets can be used in lawsuits and HR disputes, and employers monitor them. Many companies have fired people for misusing e-mail. Treat every e-message as public and archived.

**Exhibit 4.6: checklist for reviewing your own correspondence (p. 110).**
- *Opening:* a "so what?" subject line; clear, specific context; a clearly stated purpose; a specific foreshadowing statement.
- *Body:* specific, meaningful support for each point; no gaps in logic.
- *Closing:* an appropriate final paragraph with contact information and/or next steps.
- *Structure and format:* well organized and coherent; transitions between sentences and paragraphs; formatting that meets professional standards for the medium; no long dense paragraphs; "so what?" headings; effective lists and bullets.

## Chapter 5: Creating leadership documents and reports

**Minimum reader expectations for any report (p. 115):** clear, easy-to-find messages; logical, coherent organization; professional appearance and formatting; and content that is appropriate, accurate, and complete. A report can also follow a story arc: current situation, then complication, then resolution or recommendation.

### Exhibit 5.1: report types by purpose (pp. 116–117)
| Purpose | Type | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Inform | Progress | Status of tasks: done, remaining, expected delays; sometimes preliminary conclusions to test |
| Inform | Financial | Performance for reporting, such as SEC 10-K and 10-Q filings |
| Inform | Sales/marketing | Results for a period, plus prospects, trends, positioning |
| Inform | Operational | Operational performance and compliance, such as health, safety, and environment |
| Inform | Meeting minutes | Date, attendees, old and new business, action items |
| Inform | Research/investigative | Results, often with recommended actions; benchmarking, analyst reports |
| Instruct | Procedure | Steps in chronological order |
| Instruct | Policy | Rules or guidelines that govern behavior |
| Instruct | Performance appraisal | Records quality of work, for the legal record and for improvement |
| Instruct | RFP | Specifies what a proposal must contain |
| Persuade | Annual report | Financials plus mission, vision, accomplishments, plans; aimed at investors and analysts |
| Persuade | Feasibility | Argues an approach will work across economic, technical, and cultural aspects, and recommends action |
| Persuade | Proposal | Seeks acceptance of a product, service, or solution through needs and benefits; often answers an RFP; legally binding |
| Persuade | Business plan | Value proposition, feasibility, profitability, following standard templates such as the SBA's |

### Organizing reports (pp. 117–120)
- The document type can dictate the structure. Procedures are chronological. Proposals may be deductive, problem/solution, compare/contrast, or cause/effect.
- **Make the logic so obvious that no one asks where a point came from.** If you keep writing "as mentioned earlier" or "returning to point A," the structure needs fixing.
- **Default to deductive or direct order:** main message, conclusions, and recommendations first, then the support. Use inductive order for high-context cultures or hostile, resistant readers. Plan with outlines, storyboards, or the Pyramid Principle, and order the sections in the sequence the reader's questions will arise.
- **Open with power.** An executive summary or abstract holds the conclusions and recommendations. The introduction then gives context and moves straight to the recommendation and its support. A **letter of transmittal** (Exhibit 5.2) comes before the title page. It says what is attached and why, highlights the key conclusion, and offers follow-up or thanks.
- **Develop with reason.** Apply the **MECE** test to sections: no overlap, each one justified, and roughly balanced in length. If one topic runs to pages and another to a paragraph, regroup or split them. **Be selective.** Do not include analysis just because you did it. Readers want your interpretation more than the raw data, so put supporting detail in appendixes and leave out anything doubtful. Ask "so what?" of each item. Use short paragraphs, headings, lists, and clear transitions.
- **Close with grace.** Usually you state conclusions up front and end with next steps or an implementation plan. Longer reports may briefly recap the main points.

### Exhibit 5.3: formal report contents, in order (p. 121)
1. **Letter or memo of transmittal (or preface):** addressed to the decision maker. States the purpose, may highlight content, and always says what you expect the reader to do next. Includes contact information.
2. **Cover:** a title that captures the "so what," plus sender, recipient, and date.
3. **Title page:** the same as the cover. Some fields add a descriptive abstract.
4. **Table of contents:** main headings and second-level subheadings, hyperlinked in electronic versions. It should reveal a logical grouping, not a random list of analysis steps (Exhibit 5.4).
5. **Executive summary:** about 10% of the discussion's length, but as long as needed to carry the core content.
6. **Introduction/opening:** background and what prompted the report. In a direct report, it also gives conclusions and recommendations.
7. **Discussion/body:** the argument, organized logically.
8. **Closing/conclusions:** in an inductive report, the conclusions and recommendations go here. In a direct report, keep it to a short courteous close, plus implications or future research if relevant.
9. **Next steps:** actions expected of readers. These often appear in the transmittal letter instead.
10. **Appendixes:** lengthy data, qualifications, extra charts, survey instruments. Reference each one by number in the text, in the order you cite them.
11. **Bibliography:** all sources, including interviewees, in the discipline's citation style.

Add a **list of figures** after the table of contents when there are many charts (Exhibit 5.5).

### Handling exhibits (p. 123)
- Never drop in an exhibit (table, graph, diagram) without discussing it, numbering it, and titling it.
- Place it as close as possible after the text that discusses it. Use an appendix only for supplementary material.
- Number exhibits consecutively and refer to each by number just before it appears. Appendix exhibits also need numbers and titles, in the order you reference them.

### Research and citation (pp. 124–125)
Document your methods for primary research and your sources for secondary research. Use a standard style (Chicago, APA, MLA). For web sources, confirm reliability (author, title, publisher or date, affiliation). Trace a claim back to its original source, or do not use it. Cite the full URL of the specific page, not the home page. Use a short form in the text and the full citation in the bibliography. Tools such as Zotero help. Cite all quoted or paraphrased ideas immediately after the point, for three reasons: to credit the originator, to show you did the research, and to let readers find the source. Failing to do so is plagiarism.

### Progress reports (pp. 125–126)
Minimum sections: (1) introduction, (2) project description, (3) work completed, (4) work in progress, (5) work remaining, (6) overall appraisal of progress. Add issues or challenges where relevant. For simple tasks, use a table with columns for **Task, Completed, In progress, Remaining, Comments**. If a work plan exists, shade the completed portion (Exhibit 5.6). Progress reports can be formal reports or e-mails.

### Exhibit 5.7: proposal contents (p. 127)
1. **Introduction:** the problem statement and background, company and industry context (detailed research goes in other sections or an appendix), and an overview of the proposal's purpose.
2. **Needs and benefits:** a detailed assessment of the client's problem and needs, and how your solution meets them and adds value.
3. **Scope:** boundaries. What is included and excluded, the main focus, and exactly what will be delivered.
4. **Method and working relationship:** the research or analytical approach, and how you will work with the client, for example joint teams.
5. **Task and time breakdown:** phases, tasks, deadlines, responsibilities, with a Gantt or CPM chart often in an appendix, plus contingencies for "what ifs."
6. **Costs:** fees, often as tiered options priced from highest to lowest.
7. **Qualifications:** key background and capabilities, with detailed case examples or certifications in an appendix.
8. **Acceptance clause:** a signature line showing acceptance and agreement to pay, at the end or in the transmittal letter.

A proposal is a sales document that also argues you are the right party for the work, and it is usually legally binding.

### Executive summaries (p. 128; Exhibit 5.8)
**Rules:**
- It is the part most readers will actually read, so it must convey the report's substance **without the reader going further.**
- It must **stand alone.** It contains only information found in the report. It may include its own small graph, but it **must not refer to figures in the body.**
- Length is typically **no more than about 10%** of the report, longer if needed to cover the content.
- It must include the **major conclusions and recommendations plus enough support to persuade** the reader to accept them.
- Tone is direct. Style is concise but not clipped.

**Model structure (Exhibit 5.8):** an opening paragraph giving who asked, who did the work, and the question to be answered. Then message-style headed sections: methods ("Analytical Methods"), the key finding stated as a claim ("Performance Impacts Compensation") with one supporting chart whose title states the finding, and a "Recommendation" that says exactly what the client should do.

### Abstracts (pp. 128–131)
Used with research, technical, and lab reports, articles, and conference proceedings. The purpose depends on context. Internally, an abstract gives managers status, preliminary findings, and issues so they can make funding or other decisions. For publications or conferences, it draws readers or attendees and provides a record. Databases index abstracts, so use specific, searchable terms. Every abstract should be clear, concise, coherent, and self-contained. It must contain nothing that is not in the report, and it should follow the report's order.
- **Descriptive abstract:** very short. Describes what the report covers (purpose and scope) but **not** the findings or conclusions, for example "This report provides …"
- **Informative abstract:** at most about 10% of the report. Summarizes the purpose, scope, methods, **findings, and conclusions.** Example pattern: research question, phases and methods, then a bulleted list of conclusions.

### Formatting for accessibility (pp. 130–137)
- Busy readers skim and jump to the sections that concern them. Use **message-driven headings**, bulleted or numbered lists, and short paragraphs so they can find the key messages.
- **Policy and procedure documents:** organize them around who, what, when, where, and how. Example (Exhibits 5.11 and 5.12): a dense one-paragraph policy rewritten with an opening purpose statement, then "Who Is Eligible," "What Is Covered" (bullets plus exclusions), and "How to File" (numbered steps), ending with a named contact.
- **Layout:** generous white space, and a wider left margin if the document will be bound. Single-spaced with blank lines between paragraphs and no indent (academic and publisher formats differ, so follow their guidelines). Left-aligned. Consistent spacing after periods.
- **Fonts:** a serif font at 11–12 pt for report text. Do not mix serif and sans serif within a report.
- **Heading hierarchy (p. 135):** major headings bold and centered (possibly larger); first-level subheadings flush left on their own line; second-level subheadings flush left and run in with a period; third-level numbered and run in. Keep headings parallel, and avoid all caps, small caps, and underlining (which also looks like a hyperlink).
- **A heading does not replace text.** Write the following paragraph as if the heading were not there. For example, "Maximum transplant program. The maximum transplant program is …" rather than continuing from the heading with "This option …"
- **Running headers and footers** on any document longer than one page: document title, section, author, page number, and sometimes the date (Exhibit 5.13).

## Chapter 6: Leadership presentations in person and online

**The three P's process (Exhibit 6.1, p. 145).**
- **Plan:** determine the strategy, analyze the audience, choose the medium and delivery method, and set the structure.
- **Prepare:** develop the content, test the flow and logic, edit and proofread, master the content, and practice.
- **Present:** set up, build rapport, deliver, manage Q&A, and assess the results.

### Plan (pp. 145–155)
- **Strategy.** *Context:* what is on the audience's mind (organization, industry, world events) that you can connect to or must set up? *Purpose:* what must the talk achieve, and what should the audience do afterward?
- **Minimum audience questions:**
  1. What is my primary purpose with this audience?
  2. Who is the primary audience, and who are the secondary audiences?
  3. Why are they attending, and how will I motivate them to listen?
  4. What should they do as a result?
  5. How should they feel?
- *Timing:* right after lunch, people are sluggish. First thing in the morning, people arrive late. At the end of the day, they want to leave. *Feedback:* plan how you will gauge reception, such as polls, audience-response tools, or a Twitter backchannel, while remembering the backchannel carries risks.
- **Delivery methods (Exhibit 6.2):**
  - *Stand-up without visuals:* flexible and confident, but demands deep preparation.
  - *Round-table:* interactive and good for rapport, but harder to control and to keep on time.
  - *Stand-up with projection:* supports the message visually, but depends on the technology and needs a backup plan.
  - *Flipcharts:* flexible and good for recording ideas, but can look less prepared.
  - *Videoconference:* cheap and spans locations, but has lag and weaker rapport.
  - *Phone conference:* body-language cues are lost.
  - *Webcast/webinar:* cheap, recordable, and easy to collect feedback from, but impersonal. It relies on your voice, and the audience is easily distracted.
- **Choose a round-table format when you want to:** have an informal, interactive discussion; gather input; build consensus on conclusions; check facts or find missing ones; surface and resolve issues; or cover a lot of material quickly.
  - *Delivering one:* send or hand out the pack beforehand and steer people to the page you are on. Look up, including at the camera for remote attendees. (1) Direct people to the page. (2) State the exhibit's main message, which the title should already capture. (3) Explain legends, symbols, and acronyms. (4) Walk them through complex charts. (5) Give them time to scan. (6) Be flexible. (7) Give a transition before turning the page. (8) Watch body language, theirs and yours. Silence is fine.
  - *Pack format:* number the pages. Pages can be denser than projected slides, and 12-pt text is acceptable. Make it a complete deck (cover, agenda, text, and graphs) that someone who missed the meeting can understand.
- **Stand-up extemporaneous talks.** These make it easier to control the flow, keep eye contact, adapt, and look confident. Do not memorize or read a script. Do keep notes for numbers. Glance at a laptop or confidence monitor, or a printed handout with 2–6 slides per page, and never turn to read the screen.
- **Impromptu talks or elevator speeches.** Keep the current "so what?" of your projects in mind so you are ready. When called on cold: (1) pause and breathe; (2) open with your name or a brief positive remark; (3) build on an earlier speaker's point if it fits, positively; (4) pick one main message and two or three supporting points; (5) state the message and points, give the details, then restate the main points.
- **Webcasts.** Know your platform's features (audio, video, document sharing, chat, polling, recording, annotation). Best practices: build in stories and several peaks of interest, with simple visuals; share the stage so the audience hears more than one voice; have a helper run the chat and the technology; allow at least 30 minutes to set up and test; speak with energy at a steady pace with no fillers; smile, and stand if you are audio-only. On video: use a clean, well-lit backdrop; keep expressions and gestures modest, since the camera exaggerates them; do not fidget; sit upright and lean in slightly; look mostly at the camera.
- **Structure.** Outline the flow before building slides, using the Pyramid Principle or a storyboard and deciding between deductive and inductive order. Build a **ghost pack**, a set of placeholder slides with their titles, to set the flow and divide work among a team.

### Prepare (pp. 155–162)
- **Introduction.** It starts the moment you stand up, or the moment you send the invitation online. Open with a hook: a fact, quotation, example, anecdote, question, or reference to the occasion. A humorous anecdote is safer than a joke. **Never** joke about your subject, apologize for being unprepared, or fiddle with the equipment. Then use **CPF**: context, purpose, and foreshadowing of the topics. Usually state the conclusion or recommendation up front. Use an indirect order for resistant audiences or indirect cultures. On a controversial topic, briefly address the opposing view early so you seem objective, without letting it take over.
- **Agenda slide.** Tell the story in miniature: situation, then complication, then resolution. Do **not** list every slide title, and do not use empty bullets such as "Introduction," "Conclusion," or "Q&A." Every bullet should carry a "so what?" Exhibit 6.5 contrasts a 12-item laundry list with four meaningful items: our understanding of the current situation, project objectives and approach, team structure and capabilities, and next steps.
- **Conclusion.** Recap the main points, perhaps with a summary slide or the agenda again, and restate the recommendation. End strong and energetic, never "Well, that's all," and never trail off. If there is Q&A, present a summary slide *before* the Q&A, then **restate the key message after the Q&A** so the last word is yours, not the last questioner's.
- **Attention curve (Exhibit 6.6).** Attention peaks at the opening hook and again at the conclusion, and dips in the middle. So tell them what you will say, say it, and then tell them what you said. Longer talks need that repetition.
- **Body (about 80% of the talk).** Be selective and relevant. Cut anything that invites "so what?" Support each point with examples and graphics. Listeners cannot reread, so use explicit transitions and more repetition than you would in writing. Test the logic with a **one-sentence summary** that combines the main message and supporting points.
- **Test the flow.** Tell the story aloud, to yourself and to someone else, in a couple of minutes. Reorganize if it does not hold together.
- **Edit and proofread slides.** The final deck should be clear, concise, coherent, correct, and confident. Check that every slide title states the "so what?" of its chart. Trim bullets without stripping out their meaning. Check consistency in title position, fonts, and margins. Proof on paper from bottom to top, then on the projector, then in black-and-white print preview if you will hand it out.
- **Practice.** Rehearse aloud at least twice, ideally in the real room with the real equipment. Use a mirror, a video recording, or friends. Teams must rehearse together to smooth the handoffs and check timing. Check the room and the technology, or test the online setup with a partner.
- **Timing.** Respect the time limit. Running over irritates the audience, and being cut off costs you your close. Prepare two or three examples per point so you can drop some. Putting the most important points first is a fallback, but it risks an anticlimax. Rehearse with a timer and come in slightly under.

### Present (pp. 162–170)
- **Nerves.** Preparation is the main cure. Visualize success. A little adrenaline helps. The audience is forgiving and notices little. Smile, breathe, and focus on the audience rather than yourself. Find a friendly face, and chat with people beforehand. If you blank, try **"shirttailing"**: repeat your last point, which sounds like emphasis and usually brings back the next one.
- **Eye contact.** Hold a few seconds with individual people across the whole room, adjusted to the culture. Avoid darting glances, staring at one person, favoring one side, looking over heads, and reading notes. Online, lean in and look at the camera.
- **Posture and gestures.** Stand tall with feet shoulder-width apart, or sit upright leaning slightly forward online. Use natural gestures with elbows near your sides. Do not point at the screen or use a laser pointer; use slide highlighting, or stand level with the screen facing the audience. Step out from behind the podium and move with purpose.
  **Common delivery problems to avoid:** (1) slouching, (2) leaning to one side, (3) pacing, (4) clasping your hands, (5) hands on hips, (6) gripping the podium, (7) too many or unnatural gestures, (8) fidgeting with objects, (9) rocking or shifting, (10) leaning on the podium or sitting on the table.
  Review a recording with the sound off to see your body language, and in fast-forward to spot repeated gestures.
- **Voice.** Listen to an audio-only recording and count your fillers. (1) Project to the back row, and use a microphone if you are straining; speak at normal volume online. (2) Articulate, especially final syllables. (3) Do not rush; pause between ideas. (4) Vary pitch and rhythm to avoid a monotone. (5) Avoid words you trip over, or drill them. Avoid up-speak.
- **Delivering with visuals.** Keep eye contact with the audience, not the screen. Keep your volume up when changing slides. **Introduce each slide's message before you click to it.** Online, pause briefly after each transition and tell people where you are. Allow **about 2–3 minutes per slide**. Do not use automatic slide timings. Check the projected colors and font sizes from the back row.
- **Q&A.** Prepare more than you think you need, including for hostile questions. Losing your composure can overshadow the whole talk. Teams should agree in advance who answers what.
  **Tips for handling the Q&A session (p. 169):**
  1. Set and announce when you will take questions. Online, have a moderator collect them.
  2. Listen carefully.
  3. Repeat or paraphrase the question, which also buys you thinking time.
  4. Answer briefly and stop. Do not wander off on tangents.
  5. Do not bluff. Say you will find out.
  6. When one questioner tries to monopolize you, move away and re-engage the room.
  7. Answer multi-part questions in order, and offer to continue afterward.
  8. Restate your main message after the Q&A.
- **Overall effect.** Ethos decides the result. Believe in your message and prepare thoroughly. One rule of thumb: about an hour of preparation per minute of presenting, adjusted to the situation.

**Self-evaluation (Application 6.1).** Watch your recording three times: once with no sound (body language), once with sound only (voice and fillers), and once with both. Score yourself on presence, gestures, eye contact, fillers, energy, voice, introduction (CPF), organization, command of the content, conclusion, graphics quality, how you handle charts, and how well chart titles carry meaning.

## Chapter 7: Graphics with a leadership edge

**Core idea.** Visuals make a presentation more persuasive, but they must carry meaning, not decoration. The problem with PowerPoint is how people use it, not the tool. A presentation stands or falls on the quality of its content. The presenter delivers the message, and the graphics support the presenter.

### When to use graphics: six purposes (pp. 175–181)
1. **Reinforce the message,** for example a line graph showing that sales are falling. Text charts are especially important online.
2. **Give the presentation a road map.** The agenda slide should reflect the logic, like an executive summary of headings only, not a list of slide titles. For complex topics, use a framework graphic as a **tracker** that reappears at the start of each section, highlighting the current part (Exhibit 7.1). Keep the framework simple, and do not repeat it in a short talk.
3. **Show relationships and concepts:** process or flow, cycle, hierarchy, relationship, forces at work, segmentation, and so on (Exhibit 7.3).
4. **Support assertions,** quantitatively with a chart of the numbers or qualitatively with representative quotations grouped by finding (Exhibits 7.5 and 7.6).
5. **Emphasize important ideas.** People remember more of what they see than of what they hear.
6. **Maintain interest** through variety, not gimmicks. Online, the visuals may be all the audience sees, so avoid distracting animation and cluttered slides.

### Content and design principles for all charts (pp. 181–184)
1. **Keep it simple but meaningful.** Less is more, but do not strip out the meaning. Ask whether the graphic is useful and necessary. Decorative slides lose audiences, especially on audio-only webcasts.
2. **One main message per slide.** Check: What am I trying to say? If they remember one thing, is it the right one? Does every word and graphic support it? If you must continue onto another slide, repeat the title with "(continued)," but that is often a sign the content needs breaking down.
3. **The chart title states the "so what?"** Numbers do not speak for themselves. The title tells the audience how to read the data, for example "Revenue Projected to Double by 4th Q 2014," not "Revenue."

**Color.** Choose colors on purpose, considering the message, color psychology (heavy bright yellow tires the eye; blues and greens calm), and cultural meanings. **Contrast matters most:** colors are judged by how they relate to each other. In a dim room, light text on a dark background projects best. In summary: (1) keep colors simple and compatible; (2) make sure adjacent colors are easy to tell apart; (3) check that text contrasts sharply with its background; (4) make sure the colors support the image and message you want. Company logo colors are not always the best choice for a whole deck.

### Data charts (pp. 184–187)
Decide the message first, then the data that support it, then the chart type. **Tufte's principles:** show the data; keep attention on the substance, not the method or design; do not distort; fit many numbers into a small space; make large data sets coherent; invite comparison; show several levels of detail; serve a clear purpose; and integrate the chart with the text.

**Exhibit 7.7: choosing a chart type.**
| Type | Use it for | Design tips |
|---|---|---|
| Pie | Proportions or shares of a whole; good for executives and non-specialists | Start the largest slice at 12 o'clock; put numbers inside and labels outside, with no legend; outline slices for print |
| Bar/column | Absolute values, relative sizes, close comparisons, differences; works for most audiences | Rotate the y-axis label to horizontal; make gaps narrower than bars; avoid 3-D |
| Line | Trends over time and interactions between variables | Label lines directly instead of using a legend; rotate the y-axis label; limit the number of lines |
| Stacked bar | Showing differences and lots of data compactly; better for analytical audiences | Make sure segments stay distinct in black and white; align numbers and labels |
| Histogram | Frequency distributions; for statistically minded audiences, since it is not intuitive | Rotate labels; avoid a second y-axis |
| Scatter plot | Correlation, or how well a variable follows an expected pattern | Usually needs explaining, so the "so what?" title matters most; rotate the y-axis label |

**Ethics of data graphics (p. 187).** Do not distort through manipulated numbers, misleading design, the wrong chart type, or missing context. Five integrity questions:
1. Does the data set fully support my message?
2. Can I give enough context for it to be read accurately?
3. Are the numbers accurate and shown honestly?
4. Does the design distort or hide anything?
5. Are all axes and data labeled accurately and adequately?

### Text slides (Exhibit 7.8, pp. 187–188)
1. Keep the text simple but meaningful. Every word must count.
2. Use hanging indents so wrapped text aligns with text, not under the bullet.
3. Limit each bullet to one or two lines.
4. Keep bullets parallel.
5. Never use a lone bullet. Like an outline, a 1 needs a 2.
6. Vary the layout. Use two columns (for example, division and priority actions) instead of slide after slide of bullets.
7. Use simple shapes or boxes to show relationships the bullets hide, for example one division that cuts across others.
If the content cannot be made both legible and meaningful on slides, consider a handout, poster, or round-table format instead.

### Building slides in presentation software (pp. 188–195)
- **Avoid chart junk** (Exhibit 7.9): busy photo backgrounds, 3-D effects, legends, unlabeled axes, and needless zeros. Just because the software can do something does not mean you should.
- **Templates:** start from one if you like, but customize it, for example with a subtle logo, so it reflects you or your organization. Make sure the background never competes with the content.
- **Graphs imported from Excel.** Fix these common faults (Exhibits 7.12 and 7.13): a duplicate title (Excel's plus the slide's); an unrotated axis label; bars too thin (gaps should be narrower than bars); 3-D effects; repeated labels and extra zeros; a background that does not match the deck. The fixed version has one "so what?" title ("Steady Increase in Sales Since 2008"), clean axes, and no clutter.
- **Animation:**
  1. Use it only to control the pacing or to help understanding, for example revealing bullets or lines one at a time only when you will discuss each at length. Otherwise show everything at once.
  2. Never add it just because you can. Spinning logos and similar effects pull the eye away.
  3. Bring objects in from the most logical direction over the shortest distance.
  4. Stick to one or two conservative effects; "appear" is often best.
  5. Run the whole deck in slide-show view to catch stray animations.
- **Reading order.** Audiences read top-left to bottom-right and expect the main message in a title at the top. Do not bury it at the bottom or in the middle. If the design breaks that order, guide the audience through it.

**Top 10 guidelines for using graphics (p. 195).**
1. Decide on the message, then the data that support it, then how to show the data.
2. Use graphics for the right reasons: reinforcement, a road map, support for assertions.
3. Choose the right chart type for the message.
4. Keep integrity. Do not distort the data.
5. Keep graphics simple but meaningful. They should make the message easier to understand.
6. Put a "so what?" title at the top of every slide.
7. Create or customize a template that reflects the presenter or the organization.
8. Make fonts and images large enough to read from the back of the room.
9. Choose colors for contrast and conservatively, remembering that perception and cultural meaning vary.
10. Do not overuse or misuse animation.

**Practice habit (Applications 7.1–7.4).** Look at the data, decide on the comparison (part-to-whole, time series, correlation, and so on), sketch the chart, and write a title that tells the audience what to conclude. For a team web presentation, agree on the storyboard first, then create a "so what?" title for each visual and an overall title stating the main message.
