# Documents

Sources: Barrett, *Leadership Communication*, Ch. 4–5, and the CMU 17-603 lecture notes. Paraphrased. More detail is in `textbook-ch1-7.md`.

## Rules for any document

- Lead with the main point or request.
- Write for one specific reader.
- Use headings that state a finding, like "Hosting costs will double by 2027," and not just a topic, like "Costs."
- End with the specific next step: what, who, by when, and how to reach you.
- Respect length limits. Cut repetition before you shrink the font.
- Keep formatting plain: one font, black text, simple tables, numbered steps for sequences, bullets for parallel items.

## Email asking for action

1. Subject line: name the action and the topic. "Meeting request: resolving the release blockers" beats "Quick question."
2. First sentence or two: the problem and what you are asking for.
3. The problem in brief. Use a short numbered list if it has parts.
4. Why it matters to the reader: shared stakes and what they gain. If you have no authority over the readers, persuade; don't demand.
5. Details: date, time, length, place or link, and who should attend.
6. What the meeting or action will produce, such as decisions, owners, and dates.
7. Close: a reply-by date, an offer of alternatives, and your name, title, and contact details.
8. Attachments: list each one and say why the reader needs it.

Avoid casual greetings with senior readers, personal chatter, blame, vague asks, and long disclaimers.

## Memo

```
TO:       Name, title, organization
FROM:     Name or organization
DATE:     Full date
SUBJECT:  Specific topic and purpose
```

After the header comes a rule line, then the body. No salutation or signature is needed.

## Executive summary

The executive summary is the part of a report most people actually read, so it has to work on its own.

- The reader should get the substance without reading anything else.
- Include only what the full report contains. It can carry its own small table or chart but shouldn't point to figures elsewhere.
- Keep it to about 10% of the report, or the stated limit (often one page).
- Give the conclusions, the recommendation, and enough evidence to accept both.
- Keep the tone direct and the style concise without being clipped.

A one-page structure that works:

1. Header: a memo block, or a title with "Prepared for / by / date."
2. Opening paragraph, about four sentences: who asked, what question you answered, how you answered it, and your recommendation.
3. A small comparison table if you weighed options against the same criteria.
4. Three or four short sections, each under a heading that states a finding, with evidence tied to named people and real facts.
5. Next steps or phases, with timing.
6. Risks and how to handle them. If politics or personal conflict is involved, address its effect without naming the conflict in a formal document.
7. The decision you need, in one sentence.

Before sending, check that:

- every figure and name matches the source, and estimates are labeled
- the recommendation is in the first paragraph
- it fits the length limit in its final format (export to PDF to check)
- it has no AI patterns (see `ai-writing-tells.md`)

## Proposal sections

1. Introduction: the problem, the background, and the purpose.
2. Needs and benefits: the reader's problem and the value of your solution.
3. Scope: what's included, what's excluded, and what you'll deliver.
4. Method: your approach and how you'll work with the client.
5. Tasks and timeline: phases, deadlines, owners, and contingencies.
6. Costs: fees, often offered at several price points.
7. Qualifications: relevant experience.
8. Acceptance: a signature line.

A proposal is a sales document and is often binding.

## Reports

Organize around the reader's question, not around your process. Put conclusions and recommendations near the front. Use headings that state findings, short paragraphs, and lists so skimmers can find things. Place a figure right after the text that discusses it, and give it a title that states the takeaway.

## Abstracts

A descriptive abstract says what the report covers but not what it found. An informative abstract adds the findings and conclusions. Both must stand on their own, include nothing that isn't in the report, and follow the report's order.
