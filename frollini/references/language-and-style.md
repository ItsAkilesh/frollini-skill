# Language and style

Sources: Barrett, *Leadership Communication*, Ch. 3, and the CMU 17-603 lecture "The Language of Leaders." Paraphrased.

## Tone is credibility

Style shapes how readers judge you. Aim for crisp and clear, not harsh or curt. Keep your own voice; borrowed voices sound insincere.

Reread your draft as the reader. Do you sound confident? Fair? Worth reading?

## Basic habits

- Read the draft aloud.
- Ask someone else to read anything sensitive.
- Choose simple words.
- Spell out abbreviations the reader may not share.
- Use strong verbs.
- Cut empty modifiers ("really," "kind of," "possibly," "thing").
- Use qualifiers ("I feel," "probably," "in my opinion") only when the doubt is real.
- Don't phrase statements as questions.
- Use jargon only if the reader knows it.

## Be concise

Short writing takes effort. Pascal apologized for a long letter because he hadn't had time to write a shorter one. Cut every word that adds nothing, but don't compress into shorthand that loses meaning.

## Be direct

Use the active voice: "The team investigated the crash," not "The crash was investigated by the team." Passive voice is fine when the actor is unknown or unimportant, or when you need to protect them.

Don't open with "There is" or "It is." Start with the real subject: "Three bugs block the release," not "There are three bugs that are blocking the release."

Replace wordy phrases:

| Wordy | Short |
|---|---|
| due to the fact that | because |
| during the time that | while |
| for the purpose of | to |
| in the case of | in, for |
| in order to | to |
| at this point in time | now |
| over and above | beyond |
| please be advised that | (cut) |
| we are in receipt of | we received |

Drop needless "who," "which," and "that": "Jane's documentation is helpful," not "The documentation which was written by Jane is helpful."

Don't repeat yourself. If every list item starts with the same words, move those words into the lead-in sentence.

Use verbs, not nominalizations: "We will investigate the flaws to find the cause," not "We will perform an investigation of the flaws for a determination of the cause."

Prefer the plain word. Inflated vocabulary hides meaning and sounds machine-made.

## Be memorable

- Tell a short story or give a concrete example. It makes a dry point stick.
- Repeat a key phrase on purpose for emphasis. Accidental repetition looks careless; deliberate repetition persuades.
- Keep items in a list or series in the same grammatical form:

  Not parallel: "Consider the reader's motives, your bottom line should come early, and the strongest arguments first."

  Parallel: "Consider the reader's motives, state your bottom line early, and lead with your strongest argument."

## Usage rules worth following

- Use the serial comma ("A, B, and C"). It prevents ambiguity.
- Follow local punctuation conventions. In American English, commas and periods go inside quotation marks.
- Follow your organization's style guide when it has one.

The errors that distract readers most are fragments, run-ons, subject–verb disagreement, pronoun errors, misused apostrophes, missing or extra commas, too many dashes or ellipses, and wrong words (its/it's, there/their).

## Editing and proofreading

Editing asks whether the writing is clear, concise, and correct. Proofreading catches errors in grammar, spelling, and format.

- Finish the content before you edit, and edit before you proofread.
- Don't proofread while drafting.
- Leave time between writing and editing.
- Watch for your own habitual mistakes.
- Check consistency in names, numbers, terms, and formatting.
- Don't trust spell-check alone.
- Proof a printed copy if you can.
- Read the text backward, sentence by sentence or slide by slide, so you see what is on the page and not what you meant.

## Common fixes

- "It has been decided that your proposal does not meet the guidelines" → "Your proposal doesn't meet the guidelines, so we can't accept it."
- "Per your email, we are sending you the report in regard to our analysis" → "Here is our analysis, as you requested."
- "To ensure optimal conclusions, it is absolutely essential that the analysts are given any and all data necessary" → "Give the analysts all the data they need."
- An email that opens "Hey Rob..." to a senior partner, chats about vacation, wraps phrases in scare quotes, and ends with a vague ask and a long disclaimer damages the sender's credibility. State the purpose and the request at the top, in a professional tone, with no errors.
