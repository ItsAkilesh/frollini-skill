# Strategy and structure

Sources: Barrett, *Leadership Communication*, Ch. 2, and the CMU 17-603 lecture notes. Paraphrased.

## Start with purpose

Writing informs, persuades, instructs, or engages. Before drafting, write down the message and the result you want from the reader.

If you can't state those, the thinking isn't done, and the writing will be muddled too. A classic example is a manager's memo about copier misuse. The first draft threatened staff ("will not be tolerated"). Once the manager worked out what he actually wanted, it became a short, friendly note announcing pay-per-copy.

## Read the context

Ask:

- What else is happening that affects the reader?
- Is this a first notice, a follow-up, or a reply?
- What does the reader already know or believe?
- What do they expect from this medium?
- What organizational, political, or cultural factors apply?
- What else is competing for their attention?

## Plan the strategy

| Element | Questions |
|---|---|
| Purpose | What do I want to happen? Does it differ by reader? |
| Message | What is the main point? How do I frame it so this reader accepts it? What should they do? |
| Medium | Email, document, meeting, or call? What costs or logistics apply? |
| Audience | Who reads it first? Who else will see it? What's at stake for each? |
| Messenger | Am I the right person to send this? |
| Timing | When should it arrive, and in what order with other messages? |
| Feedback | How will I know it worked? |

## Know the reader

- Who is the main reader? What do they expect, and how do they feel about the topic?
- Who else will read it, and what do they want?
- What do I want them to do and to feel?
- How expert are they? Executives want the conclusion and the decision first. Technical readers want the method and evidence. General readers need context and no jargon. Mixed groups need a top-line summary plus detail they can skip.
- What relationships are in play: who reports to whom, who has history, who loses something?

## Organize for the reader

Get to the main point immediately, in most cases. Stay on topic. Show why it matters to this reader. Arrange the content so the reader can find what they need and decide.

## The Pyramid Principle

Barbara Minto's method organizes ideas from the top down:

- The top is one governing statement: your answer, recommendation, or request.
- The middle is two to four supporting points, each answering "why?" or "how?" about the top.
- The bottom is the evidence for each supporting point.

Rules:

1. Groups don't overlap, and together they cover the argument. This is called MECE: mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive.
2. Each level summarizes the ideas below it.
3. Ideas in a group are the same kind of idea, in a logical order.
4. Use each fact once. Drop facts that don't support the top statement.
5. Test the logic by reading it as a sentence: "[Top] because [point 1], [point 2], and [point 3]."

### Building a pyramid from scattered facts

1. List every fact.
2. Sort related facts into groups.
3. Name each group. The names become your supporting points.
4. Ask what the groups add up to. That becomes the top statement.
5. Check for overlap and gaps, then order the groups, usually strongest first.

The same facts can support different top statements for different readers. Build the pyramid for the reader you have.

## Spoken versions

For talks, use the old rule: tell them what you'll say, say it, then tell them what you said. Start with the main argument and move down into detail.
