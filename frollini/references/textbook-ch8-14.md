# Barrett, Leadership Communication: Chapters 8-14 (practical guidance)

Paraphrased notes for a leader-communicator. Page numbers refer to the printed book.

## Chapter 8: Emotional intelligence and interpersonal skills for leaders

### Overview (pp. 199-200)
- Emotional intelligence (EI, or EQ) is the ability to recognize and manage emotions in yourself and in others. Interpersonal skills are EI in action: EI is what happens inside you, and interpersonal skills are how it shows when you deal with people.
- The book cites research linking EI to job performance, negotiation and leadership, and putting people skills ahead of technical knowledge for long-term career success. A leader's emotions also spread and shape the climate of the group.
- The chapter's goal is to read the "emotional subtext," the meaning beneath the words. It covers EI and how to build it, how personality relates to EI, and nonverbal communication and listening.


### Why it matters (pp. 201-204)
- A leader's emotional intelligence (EI), or the lack of it, shows in their messages. Angry, threatening, passive-voice memos ("cannot and will not be tolerated") signal low empathy and a top-down culture. Inclusive "we" language signals a community. Before sending anything, ask what the tone says about you and your organization.
- EI covers four abilities: understanding and expressing yourself, understanding and relating to others, controlling strong emotions and impulses, and adapting and solving personal or social problems.
- Mayer and Salovey describe three branches of EI (Exhibit 8.1). Each shows up in a different way:
  - **Appraisal and expression** (shown through communication): verbal and nonverbal expression, reading others' nonverbals, empathy.
  - **Regulation** (shown through control): of your own emotions and of others'.
  - **Utilization** (shown through action): flexible planning, creative thinking, motivating others, redirecting attention.
- Goleman, Boyatzis and McKee group the competencies into four domains (Exhibit 8.2). Effective leaders are strong in about six of them, not all.
  - **Self-awareness:** emotional self-awareness, accurate self-assessment, self-confidence.
  - **Self-management:** self-control, transparency, adaptability, achievement, initiative, optimism.
  - **Social awareness:** empathy, organizational awareness (reading politics and decision networks), service.
  - **Relationship management:** inspiration, influence, developing others through feedback, acting as a change catalyst, conflict management, building bonds, teamwork.

### Building self-awareness (pp. 204-209)
- Check in with yourself several times a day: What am I feeling? What do I want? How am I acting? What judgments am I making? What are my senses telling me?
- Combine self-assessments with 360-degree feedback so that you see yourself the way others see you.
- Personality profiles such as FIRO-B, the Five Factors and the MBTI help you understand yourself and others. The MBTI has four dichotomies (Exhibit 8.3):
  - E/I: where you get energy
  - S/N: how you take in information
  - T/F: how you decide
  - J/P: how you approach work
- How to use type knowledge:
  - **Lateness and missed deadlines:** if a Perceiver keeps missing deadlines, don't take it as disrespect. Treat it as a performance issue and coach time management.
  - **Teams of Introverts:** pair them up for tasks.
  - **A quiet Introvert among Extraverts:** draw them in with direct, specific questions.
  - **Virtual teams:** let Extraverts lead discussions and present. Give Introverts offline channels and private feedback.
- Caution: a profile suggests how someone is motivated. It does not predict behavior. Never use it as a label or as an excuse for poor performance.
- Thinking-type leaders are not worse at EI, but they may need to work harder at empathy and at reading facial cues.

### Five steps to improve EI (p. 209)
1. Picture the effective leader you want to become.
2. Find out who you are now through 360-degree feedback, especially from peers and direct reports.
3. Write a plan to close the gap.
4. Rehearse the new behaviors until they become automatic.
5. Get help from others. Don't try to do this alone.

### Nonverbal communication (pp. 211-215)
- Nonverbal cues carry an estimated 65-93% of meaning. The categories are:
  - appearance
  - paralanguage (volume, pitch, rate)
  - kinesics (gestures, posture)
  - eye behavior
  - proxemics (distance and seating)
  - facial expression
  - smell
  - use of time
- Only six facial expressions read the same in every culture: happiness, fear, sadness, surprise, anger and disgust. Most other signals depend on culture and context, and people overrate their ability to read them.
- **Nonverbals that can hurt your credibility (ethos):**
  - smiling too much, or never
  - gestures that contradict your words
  - shrinking or slouching
  - sitting back from the table
  - mirroring others too obviously
  - head tilts or raised eyebrows that read as doubt
  - eye contact that is wrong for the culture
  - laptops or briefcases placed on the table as barriers
  - looking at your phone
  - a fingertip-only handshake (aim for firm, web to web)
- **How to improve:**
  1. Learn the nonverbal norms of the cultures you work with. Many common U.S. hand gestures (thumbs up, OK, V sign) give offense elsewhere (Exhibit 8.4).
  2. Don't read meaning into behavior out of context. If someone's behavior matters to you or the organization, ask them about it instead of assuming. Assumptions feed office rumors.
  3. Watch for mismatches between what people say and how they say it.
  4. Video yourself:
     - Watch with the sound off to check posture, eye contact and gestures.
     - Listen without the picture to check volume, pace, filler words and pauses.
     - Strong speakers use silence and avoid "uh."
     - Dress, seating and office layout also send messages.

### Listening (pp. 215-219)
- Listening is roughly 40-45% of communication. Burley-Allen describes three levels:
  - Level 1, empathetic listening: no judgment, full attention.
  - Level 2: hearing the words but missing the intent.
  - Level 3: listening in spurts, or pretending to listen.
- **Barriers:**
  - a boring topic
  - disagreeing and planning your rebuttal
  - caring more about what you want to say
  - distractions and devices
  - preconceptions about the speaker or topic
  - emotional triggers
  - being distracted by the speaker's delivery or appearance
  - selective hearing
- **Ten habits:**
  1. Stop talking.
  2. Quiet your internal chatter.
  3. Don't multitask.
  4. Empathize.
  5. Don't interrupt; ask questions to clarify.
  6. Make eye contact suited to the culture, but no "power stare."
  7. Ignore delivery and appearance.
  8. Listen for ideas, not just facts.
  9. Keep an open mind.
  10. Notice nonverbals and what goes unsaid.
- **Show that you are listening (Exhibit 8.5):**
  - Nonverbal attending: eye contact, leaning in, nodding.
  - Verbal attending: short cues such as "I see."
  - Questions: open (how, what, could) and closed (is, did).
  - Focusing: notice whether the talk is about the speaker, the topic or someone else.
  - Reflecting: restate the facts or feelings you heard.
  - Summarizing: recap where you left off, mid-discussion and at the end.
- **Practice drills:**
  - Summarize right after a conversation or talk.
  - Take notes on main ideas, not verbatim.
  - Paraphrase silently while others speak.
  - Summarize a news story after hearing it.

## Chapter 9: Diversity and intercultural communication

### Core ideas (pp. 222-227)
- Intercultural competence means using verbal and nonverbal communication appropriately and effectively in any cultural context. It draws on emotional intelligence.
- **The "iceberg of differences"** (Exhibit 9.1, used by Shell):
  - Visible above the waterline: gender, race, age, ability, language.
  - Hidden below it: values, learning style, religion, education, life experience, thought process, work style, function and similar factors.
- Culture is a shared, fuzzy set of attitudes, beliefs, conventions and values. It shapes how people *interpret the meaning* of others' behavior. Culture is learned and shared by a group. Personality is individual.
- **Hofstede's layers of culture** (Exhibit 9.2): national, regional/ethnic/religious/linguistic, gender, generation, social class, and organizational. Differences exist within a single country too: a direct, fast-paced New Yorker communicates differently from a more indirect Texan.
- Generational stereotypes (Exhibit 9.3) are rough generalizations only. They are not accurate for individuals.
- **Three dangers when discussing culture:**
  - erasing individual uniqueness
  - stereotyping
  - ethnocentric misreading
- Use frameworks with care. Stay open, flexible and nonjudgmental. Real competence comes from direct experience, not reading.

### Before a cross-cultural encounter (p. 228)
Look up the local do's and taboos (Axtell's guides cover these):
- dining and invitation customs
- gifts
- greetings
- which topics are acceptable in business versus social settings
- gestures to avoid

### Seven cultural variables (Exhibit 9.4, pp. 228-238)
The framework adapts O'Hara-Devereaux and Johansen, plus Hall, Hofstede and Inglehart. Use these variables to analyze an audience.

| Variable | Spectrum | Implications and diagnostic questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Context** | High context (Japan, China, Korea, Arab countries, Africa) to low context (Germany, Switzerland, Scandinavia, North America) | High context: meaning lives in relationships, nonverbals and what goes unsaid; trust, face-saving and harmony matter; communication is indirect; deals take longer; a "maybe" may mean no. Low context: explicit words, facts, directness, bottom-line focus; contracts are fixed. Direct communicators can come across as rude to indirect ones (Exhibit 9.6). |
| **Information flow** | Open or controlled; up, down or across | How fast do messages travel? Directly or through channels? How much context is needed? How should information be sequenced and packaged? Words or graphics? Direct or indirect organization? Written or oral? Is informality OK in meetings? |
| **Time** | Monochronic (linear, scheduled, "time is money") to polychronic (fluid, people before schedules); also orientation to past, present or future | Is promptness expected? Is multitasking normal? How seriously are deadlines taken? Is the focus on tasks or people? What are normal work and meal hours? |
| **Language** | First language and fluent languages; also jargon of professions and genders | A little language knowledge can be dangerous. Hire your own interpreter for negotiations even if you feel fairly fluent. |
| **Power and equality** | Low power distance (U.S., UK, non-Latin Europe) to high power distance (Latin, Asian, African countries) | See Exhibit 9.7. Low power distance: subordinates expect to be consulted; the ideal boss is a "resourceful democrat"; status symbols are frowned on. High power distance: people expect to be told what to do; the ideal boss is a "benevolent autocrat"; centralization is normal. Ask: How are titles treated? Is challenging authority acceptable? Is the organization flat or layered? Who decides? How is status shown? What are attitudes to women, to age, and to social media? |
| **Collectivism vs. individualism** | "I" vs. "we" | Individualists want to know what a message means for them personally. Collectivists want to know what it means for the group. |
| **Spirituality and tradition** | Traditional/religious vs. secular-rational (Inglehart) | Is there a belief in an outside source of guidance? What are the predominant religious influences? What are the daily practices and holidays (for example, Friday as the day off in the Middle East)? These shape how people respond to messages from authority. |

What leadership looks like varies by culture. A behavior that reads as leadership in one culture can read as tyranny in another.

### Seven rules for connecting across cultures (pp. 239-240)
1. Be open and respectful. Find out what respect looks like to the other person.
2. Know the social customs: handshakes, business cards, dining.
3. Learn as much as is reasonable about the culture, its history and its language. Use an interpreter for business decisions unless you are fluent.
4. Get pointers and feedback from members of the culture.
5. Be patient and flexible, and allow time to build relationships, especially in high-context cultures.
6. Keep a sense of humor, including about yourself.
7. Use simple language. Avoid jargon, idioms, slang, and sports or war metaphors.

Applying the framework (Application 9.2): before writing to a counterpart from another culture, profile them on each variable. Then choose the channel, structure, directness and tone to match.

## Chapter 10: High-performing team leadership

### Team basics (pp. 247-249)
- Katzenbach and Smith's team basics (Exhibit 10.1) have three outcomes: performance results, collective work products and personal growth.
- A real team has three characteristics:
  - **Complementary skills:** technical/functional, problem solving and interpersonal.
  - **Accountability:** mutual and individual, with a small number of people. Large teams split into subteams and lose accountability.
  - **Commitment:** specific goals, a common approach and a meaningful purpose.
- Focus on performance and the basics, not on "becoming a team" through team-building exercises.
- The leader's job is to tell members, one by one and as a group, why they were chosen, what the team's purpose is and what is expected of them.

### Team launch and charter (pp. 249-253)
- Hold an official launch. The main causes of team conflict are unclear goals and purpose and an unclear approach to the work.
- **A team charter has four parts:**
  1. **Purpose and goals.** Write them down so that everyone can see and agree to them. Goals must be specific, measurable and tied to the purpose.
  2. **Roles and responsibilities.** Assign them at the first meeting:
     - leader, facilitator, timekeeper and note taker (decide whether these rotate)
     - a meeting coordinator
     - a work plan manager
  3. **Ground rules.** The whole team creates them and they go in the charter. Fit them to the organization's culture, and check them periodically. Topics to cover (Exhibit 10.2):
     - leadership
     - communication
     - participation
     - work products (complete and on time)
     - conflict (debate content openly; the facilitator handles disruptive conflict)
     - preparation
     - attendance
     - timing
     - decision making (consensus, with a vote or tabling if there is an impasse)
     - laptops and phones
  4. **Communication protocol.** Agree on:
     - what triggers communication
     - when to contact one member versus the whole team
     - off-limits hours
     - who speaks for the team internally and externally (often a single contact with senior management)
     - whether to send regular updates or only issues
     - which channel to use for routine messages, deliverables and emergencies
     - who receives problems and concerns
     - what goes in the shared workspace, and who posts it
- A "parking lot" holds important topics that are not on the agenda (Exhibit 10.3, sample charter).

### Action plans and work plans (pp. 253-255)
- **Action plan:** 3-5 phases that map to the goals. List the major activities and timing for each phase. More than five phases is hard to manage.
- **Work plan:** under each phase, list action items with an owner, an *end product* and a deadline. Repeat the goals at the top. Defining the end product prevents later task conflict.

### Producing team documents and presentations (pp. 254-258)
- **Single scribe:**
  - Gives consistent style.
  - Concentrates control and workload in one person, who may feel undervalued.
  - Make it work: specify exactly what each person hands the scribe and in what state of completion, have everyone review drafts, credit the scribe, and build compilation time into the plan.
- **Multiple writers:**
  - Faster, with more ownership, and authors know their content.
  - Risks overlap, gaps and an uneven style.
  - Make it work: divide sections using a pyramid or storyboard with no overlap; the top-level pyramid topics become the section headings or agenda.
- **Format and final review:** fix the template, fonts and layout up front. At the end, the whole team reads the full document and one person does the final proofread.
- **Version control:** agree on due dates, the circulation order and a naming scheme (for example, date and time in the footer and filename, removed in the final). In shared online documents, decide when to save prior versions.
- **Plan the writing steps in detail.** Don't write "create report" as a single line item. Teams underestimate this work. List the steps:
  1. Set the communication strategy.
  2. Choose the medium.
  3. Choose the format or template.
  4. Write an outline, storyboard or ghost deck.
  5. Divide up the sections.
  6. Write the first draft.
  7. Merge the sections.
  8. Test for coherence.
  9. Rewrite and edit.
  10. Read it through as a team.
  11. Proofread.
  12. Rehearse the presentation, or publish the document.

### Learning and keeping teams out of trouble (pp. 258-259)
- Run periodic process checks to revisit roles, ground rules and the communication protocol, and hold a debrief at the end. The leader gives feedback to each member and asks for feedback on their own leadership.
- **Checklist (adapted from Bens):**
  1. Hold a launch and write a charter.
  2. Get training in facilitation, meetings, problem solving and conflict.
  3. Post the ground rules.
  4. Teach the stages of team development: forming, storming, norming, performing.
  5. Anticipate roadblocks.
  6. Schedule regular peer feedback.
  7. Get feedback to the leader.
  8. Build in process checks.

### The people side (pp. 259-262)
- At the first meeting, share five things: each person's position and outside workload, past team experiences (good and bad), expectations and personal goals, personality, and cultural background.
- This exchange speeds up trust and group EI. Druskat and Wolff: build norms that create trust, group identity and group efficacy.
- Watch for members whose goals don't match the team's, such as people seeking management visibility or only a grade.
- Exhibit 10.6 lists cultural friction points on teams:
  - High-context members may want social time and may find email limiting.
  - Some members may resent having a single contact with management.
  - Some treat information as power and hoard it.
  - Polychronic members may chafe at linear work plans.
  - Joking and jargon can exclude people.
  - In high power distance cultures, people may not accept a peer as team leader.

### Team conflict (pp. 262-268)
- **Four types:**
  - **Analytical:** substantive disagreement. This is constructive, so encourage it with a ground rule that invites dissent, and separate people from issues.
  - **Task:** missed or incomplete work. Prevent it with clear end products.
  - **Interpersonal:** personality, values or style.
  - **Roles:** leadership, scope or power. Prevent it by clarifying roles up front.
- **Three approaches.** Choose based on the type of conflict and the personalities involved; for example, two Introverts in a personality clash rarely sort it out alone.
  1. **One on one.** Each person states the facts neutrally ("white hat"). Each listens without interrupting, using time limits if needed. Each explains the impact on the team. Then they propose solutions, agree, write the agreement down and sign it.
  2. **Facilitation** (often best). The facilitator:
     1. Explores all perspectives.
     2. Sets a rule of no interruptions.
     3. Has each party paraphrase the other.
     4. Addresses concerns openly and "translates" them.
     5. Confirms the translation.
     6. Invites constructive feedback.
     7. Intervenes firmly on personal attacks.
     8. Closes by stating what was agreed and the next steps.
  3. **Whole team.** Pick one facilitator. For a slacker, have one member talk to them first, then a single spokesperson at the team meeting; avoid seating that looks like an inquisition. For broader issues:
     1. List concerns as facts.
     2. Tie the conflict to the team's purpose.
     3. Agree on the main issue.
     4. Stay on the facts.
     5. Check whether the ground rules solve it.
     6. Write down the agreements and update the ground rules.
     7. Set a fallback plan.
- **Mistakes that escalate conflict:**
  - forcing people to choose from limited options
  - leaning on outsiders to resolve it
  - ignoring it
  - letting members give in and then act like victims
  - discussing team issues outside the team

### Virtual teams (pp. 268-273)
- **Benefits:** lower cost, faster schedules, access to expertise, and global reach.
- **Challenges:**
  - loss of context and nonverbal cues
  - amplified cultural differences
  - difficulty sharing complex information
  - trust
- **Remedies:**
  - Train people on the technology and on diversity.
  - Use a shared workspace or "team room."
  - Use *more* structure than a co-located team: a charter, a work plan and a communication protocol.
  - Choose meeting modes deliberately (Exhibit 10.8): same or different time crossed with same or different place.
  - Meet in person at least once, ideally at the launch, and exchange the five people topics.
- **Trust** grows from seeing follow-through and expertise. Deliver on promises on time, respect others' schedules, be straightforward, and respond promptly.
- **McKinsey's three needs for virtual teams:** shared beliefs, a "storehouse" of credibility and trust, and a shared workspace.

### Leadership communication tasks by phase (Exhibit 10.9)

| Phase | Tasks |
|---|---|
| Getting started | Select members and explain why they were chosen; set and confirm the charter; clarify roles; set ground rules; set the communication protocol |
| Doing the work | Facilitate meetings; give positive and negative feedback; coordinate and keep everyone informed; resolve conflict; update outsiders |
| Delivering | Prepare and deliver the report or presentation; debrief; close out the team |

**Peer feedback round (Application 10.2):**
- Each person gets one or two strengths and one or two areas to improve, each with an example.
- The recipient only listens, asks clarifying questions if needed, and says thank you.
- Keep it confidential to the team.
- Rating criteria: attitude, fair share of work, participation, cooperation, team play, contribution, listening, problem solving, synthesis, dependability.

## Chapter 11: Meetings: leadership and productivity

Executives spend 40-50% of their time in meetings, and as much as half of that time is seen as unproductive. Meetings also pass on the culture: bad meetings send bad messages about the organization.

### Decide whether a meeting is the right forum (pp. 282-283)
- **Start with purpose: inform, persuade, instruct or engage.**
  - If the goal is to inform, check whether email, the intranet or a post would work better.
  - If the goal is to instruct, check whether an online module would work better.
- **Consider the audience:** Is the culture mostly online or mostly face to face? Are people mostly Introverts or Extraverts?
- **Questions to ask:**
  - What exactly do I want to accomplish?
  - Is a meeting more efficient and effective than the alternatives?
  - Can I describe the outcome precisely?
  - Is this group more productive when it meets?
- **Meetings held out of habit:** if the real purpose is motivation, networking or camaraderie, say so. A social event might work better.

### Plan the meeting (pp. 284-288)
Answer six questions: purpose and outcome, agenda, attendees, setting, timing, and the information needed.
- **Purpose and end products:**
  - Give the meeting one main purpose (inform, persuade, instruct, solve a problem or decide).
  - Write out the objectives and state them at the start.
  - Pair each objective with a *tangible end product* (Exhibit 11.1). For example, "identify obstacles" produces "a list of roadblocks."
  - If you can't name an end product, the objective isn't clear enough for a meeting.
- **Agenda (Exhibit 11.2):** include:
  - date, location, who called the meeting, facilitator and note taker
  - pre-reading and what to bring
  - the objectives
  - timed topics, each with an owner and an approach (presentation, brainstorm and so on)
  - extra context, such as "first of two meetings; come ready to contribute"

  Estimate the time for each topic realistically, add at least 5 minutes per topic for transitions, and anticipate delays.
- **Attendees:** invite:
  - the decision makers
  - the budget holder
  - the people who must carry out the decisions
  - the experts
  - representatives of those affected

  Ask: Who supplies input? Who must accept the decision for it to be implemented? Who is needed to reach the decision? Who must understand it? Who needs to feel included?
- **Setting:**
  - Hold important meetings off-site, or protect on-site time with ground rules.
  - Match the room to the meeting: interactive sessions need whiteboards and seating where people can see each other; presentations need a projector and clear sightlines.
  - Use seating strategically: if you expect hostility, mix team members with the audience. Respect cultures that seat people by rank.
  - Set up virtual connections well in advance.
- **Timing:**
  - Avoid times when people are not at their best, such as Monday at 8 a.m.
  - Groups stay attentive for about 60-90 minutes, and less in virtual meetings.
  - For longer meetings, add breaks and vary the activities.
  - Consider whether collaborative workspaces could replace a long meeting.
- **Information:** send the agenda a few days ahead and bring printed copies. In virtual meetings, make sure everyone is looking at the same version, with the relevant columns highlighted.

### Run the meeting (pp. 288-297)
- **Announce the decision-making approach up front:** leader decides, vote or consensus. It usually follows the organization's culture. State it whenever it differs between meetings.
- **Assign roles:** leader, facilitator, note taker and timekeeper.
  - Keep leader and facilitator separate when you can: the leader owns content, the facilitator owns process.
  - Don't assign roles by stereotype (for example, always making a woman the note taker).
  - Don't make a habitual critic the facilitator of a brainstorm.
- **Ground rules:**
  - Set them for every meeting, write them where everyone can see them, make them actionable, and *enforce* them. Unenforced rules are an empty exercise.
  - Make unwritten norms explicit for newcomers (for example, "we always start 10 minutes late").
  - **For virtual meetings:**
    - Introduce yourself when you join.
    - Say your name before you speak.
    - No side conversations.
    - Keep the speakerphone near whoever is talking.
    - Mute your line; avoid tapping pens and shuffling papers.
- **Ten shared problem-solving tools.** A shared toolkit gives you efficiency and creativity; you can simply say "let's use the hats."
  1. **Brainstorming.** Everyone contributes. No judging. Record every idea verbatim where all can see it. Quantity over quality. Stop when ideas run out or time is up. Then sort or rank the results.
  2. **Ranking or rating.** Narrow the list using criteria the group agrees on (for example, 20 ideas down to the top 5 by ability to implement).
  3. **Sorting by category.** Test the categories with MECE: mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. This links to the pyramid principle.
  4. **De Bono's Six Thinking Hats** (Exhibit 11.3). Everyone wears the same hat at once ("parallel thinking"), which separates ego from problem solving. Cover all six:
     - White: facts
     - Red: emotions and intuition
     - Yellow: benefits
     - Black: risks, devil's advocate
     - Green: alternatives and creativity
     - Blue: process control
  5. **Opposition analysis.** Pros against cons, or "is / is not" for key words. For example, define what "world-class" does and does not mean in a vision statement.
  6. **Decision trees.** Break a problem into parts, or map risks and uncertainties.
  7. **From/to analysis.** For each dimension that must change, write the current state and the target state. It works well with McKinsey's 7S (Exhibit 11.4).
  8. **Force-field analysis (Lewin).** Describe the problem, the desired end state, the driving forces and the restraining forces. The restraining forces often reveal the root cause. For example, team competition can come from individual-only compensation (Exhibit 11.5).
  9. **2x2 matrix.** Label the axes with the deciding criteria (for example, value against difficulty) and plot the options (Exhibit 11.6).
  10. **Frameworks** (7S, 4 Ps and so on). They give shorthand and a visual structure. Adapt them to the situation; don't force the fit.

### Handle meeting problems (pp. 297-303)
The leader plans, supplies the content and anticipates problems. The facilitator protects the process.

**Common problems and fixes (Exhibit 11.7):**

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Confused objectives | Agenda with objectives and end products, sent ahead and reviewed at the start |
| Unclear roles | Name roles in the agenda or at the start |
| Process mixed up with content | Separate leader and facilitator; call a process time-out as soon as confusion appears |
| Drifting off topic | Restate the objectives; take the topic offline, put it on the next agenda, or park it visibly and revisit it at the end |
| Data overload | Control versions of handouts; use slim data packs; cut anything off-objective |
| Repetition and wheel spinning | Steer back to the objectives |
| Time violations | Start on time (late starts teach that time is flexible); use a timekeeper; if overruns recur, rework the agenda and add cushion |

- **Negativity** (Exhibit 11.8), such as "that won't work here," "not in the budget" or "another management fad," kills brainstorming.
  - Ban it with a ground rule.
  - If someone persists, confront it directly in the meeting.
  - If it continues, the leader gives private feedback afterward and looks for the underlying cause.
- **Resistance to ideas** is subtler, because it comes as counter-ideas and roadblocks. Devil's advocacy can be useful. If the resistance becomes disruptive, use four moves (Exhibit 11.9):
  - Verify: "What I understand you to be saying is..."
  - Clarify: ask them to explain it another way.
  - Align: look at the problem from their point of view.
  - Probe: "Tell me more about your concerns."
- **Conflict modes (Thomas-Kilmann, plotting assertiveness against cooperation; Exhibit 11.10):**
  - **Collaborating** is best: both sides assert and cooperate, so both win.
  - **Compromising** is acceptable when it is the only way forward, but leaves both sides only partly satisfied.
  - **Competing, avoiding and accommodating** are short-term fixes only. Competing creates a loser that others sympathize with. Avoiding buries the problem, which later explodes or leads to sabotage. Accommodating too often breeds resentment and withdrawal.
- **Other quick de-escalation moves:**
  - Turn the question back to the group.
  - Use is/is not or pro/con.
  - List points of agreement and disagreement.
  - Surface the underlying assumptions.
  - Switch to facts only (the white hat), which removes emotion.
- **Culture and personality (Exhibit 11.11):**
  - High-context attendees expect some small talk first.
  - People used to top-down information flow may hold back in brainstorms.
  - Polychronic attendees may find timekeepers and strict agendas artificial.
  - Banter and joking can exclude non-U.S. attendees.
  - High power distance attendees expect the most senior person to run the meeting and may be confused by a separate facilitator.
  - Judgers want an agenda and resent latecomers; Perceivers tend to arrive late.
  - Make room for Introverts, because Extraverts dominate.

### Make meetings lead to action (pp. 303-304)
"Nothing happens afterward" is one of the deadly sins of meetings. Four steps prevent it:
1. **Assign each task to a named person, not to the group.** Group assignments diffuse accountability.
2. **Review all actions, owners and next steps before closing.** Never let a meeting just stop.
3. **Send a summary or minutes** listing the main topics and every next step with its owner and due date. You don't need a verbatim record.
4. **Follow up soon** to confirm understanding and offer help. Check again near the deadline with anyone who habitually misses deadlines. This is good leadership, not micromanagement.

- **Evaluate the meeting with a plus/delta review:** what worked, and what to change (Application 11.3).
- **Sample problem-solving agenda flow:**
  1. Agree on objectives, end products, agenda, roles and the decision approach.
  2. Brainstorm.
  3. Group the ideas and rank or vote.
  4. Agree on an action plan.
  5. Set next steps.
  6. Review the process.

## Chapter 12: Leadership communication in an organizational context

### Organizational theory, briefly (pp. 308-316)
- **Classical approaches** share three traits: clear top-down authority, formal rules, and impersonality.
  - **Taylor (scientific management):** efficiency, close supervision, the organization as a machine.
  - **Fayol (administrative):** strict hierarchy and command and control. The manager's five functions are planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating and controlling. "Fayol's bridge" lets peers at the same level talk directly instead of going up and down the chain (Exhibit 12.1).
  - **Weber (bureaucracy):** rules, specialization and rational-legal authority. Weber contrasted it with charismatic and traditional authority.
- **Human relations approach:** began with the Hawthorne studies, which found that attention and participative supervision raised productivity more than physical conditions did.
  - McGregor's Theory X treats workers as parts that need commanding. Theory Y says workers want self-direction, participation and recognition.
  - Theory Y practices: decentralize and delegate, enlarge jobs, consult employees, and let people set their own targets in appraisals.
  - This approach produced today's flat and matrix structures with self-managed teams (Exhibits 12.2-12.3), where communication flows up, down and across.
- **Leaders versus managers:**
  - Bennis lists contrasts such as "administers vs. innovates" and "does things right vs. does the right thing." The author argues some of these unfairly belittle managers.
  - Kotter: management brings order (planning, organizing, staffing, control); leadership copes with change by setting direction and a vision. The two complement each other. Managers lead, and leaders manage, and both succeed through communication and motivation.

### Redding's ten postulates of organizational communication (p. 317)
1. Meaning is created by the receiver; it is not transferred.
2. Anything, verbal or nonverbal, can be a message.
3. Listening and input are part of communication.
4. Only the message *received* counts, because it drives action.
5. Feedback is essential, and messages can help or harm.
6. Every important communication costs money, energy and emotion.
7. Repetition is necessary but costly; balance the two.
8. Overload is inevitable because people have limited capacity.
9. Each relay through another person distorts the message.
10. Climate (culture) matters more than technique.

### What motivates people today (pp. 318-319)
Pay is not the main motivator. The research cited:
- Maslow's hierarchy of needs.
- Hawthorne: personal attention.
- Herzberg: interesting and challenging work and growing responsibility.
- Amabile and Kramer: good managers
  - provide help, resources and time
  - respond to success and failure with learning rather than judgment
  - set clear goals and explain why the work matters
  - recognize real progress
  - collaborate as a peer
  - make work more enjoyable
  - give emotional support

### Supervisor-subordinate relationships (pp. 319-327)
- **Style problems:**
  - Micromanaging makes people feel smothered and distrusted.
  - Undermanaging makes them feel abandoned.
  - Both often come from how the manager was managed before, or from personality. For example, INTJs tend to undermanage. Feeling-type subordinates need more support than a reserved Thinking-type boss may offer. An introverted boss's reserve can be misread as dislike.
- **Five factors shape the relationship:** power, trust, communication, culture, and values/ethics.
- **Power and managing up:**
  - Identify which power sources your boss relies on: legitimate, expert, reward or coercive, referent, information, or connection.
  - Identify their decision style (charismatic, thinker, skeptic, follower, controller; see Ch. 2) and tailor your approach. A "thinker" wants organized facts and figures, with likely questions answered in advance.
- **How subordinates build trust from day one:**
  - be punctual and use time well
  - be prepared, with polished deliverables delivered on time
  - ask the right questions at the right time, without being needy
  - show good interpersonal skills
  - communicate appropriately
  - show energy and enthusiasm
  - don't add to the boss's workload
- **Delegation ("keep the monkey off your back," Oncken and Wass):**
  - Schedule appointments to deal with problems people bring you.
  - Specify the level of initiative you expect.
  - Agree on status updates.
  - Check your own motives.
  - Develop people's skills.
  - Focus on results, not process.
  - Delegate to the lowest possible level.
  - Explain assignments and provide resources.
  - Build trust.

  Subordinates should act on their own and then update the boss, either routinely or right away depending on the boss's expectations. Don't wait to be told.
- **Communication:**
  - At the start of the relationship, agree on the boss's preferred channel, frequency and scope. Does everything go through them, or can you contact peers and senior people directly?
  - The boss should communicate instructions, expectations, policies, reviews and feedback.
  - The subordinate should communicate progress and issues, policy questions, improvement ideas, and any ethical violations they witness.
- **Culture:**
  - Follow the organization's formality. New hires should start conservative in dress and tone until they learn the norms.
  - Do a quick Ch. 9 variable analysis whenever you enter a new culture. For example, high power distance staff may give only positive 360-degree feedback.
- **Building an ethical climate** (eight steps):
  1. Set high standards and repeat them in public and private.
  2. Keep communication open.
  3. Be consistent and transparent.
  4. Act quickly and consistently on violations.
  5. Encourage candor both ways.
  6. Listen to complaints without judging the person raising them.
  7. Get out and interact with employees at all levels.
  8. In a crisis, be open, honest and accessible.

  Climates that breed misconduct include distrust of whether management and coworkers will keep promises, poor information from leaders, and rewards for success achieved by questionable means.
- **When to report a concern (Trevino and Nelson).** Report it if the issue:
  - harms a stakeholder or violates their rights
  - could damage your reputation or the organization's
  - breaks a law
  - fails the front-page test

  About 60% of managers' ethical issues involve HR fairness: unequal pay or bonuses, inconsistent evaluation criteria, favoritism (Exhibit 12.4).

### Mentoring (pp. 327-328)
- **Mentor:**
  - Share experience and stories, including mistakes.
  - Keep communication open.
  - Help the protégé identify problems and walk through problem solving.
  - Criticize constructively and supportively.
  - Assign homework if useful.
  - Make referrals.
  - Be honest about the limits of your expertise.
  - Ask for feedback.
  - Come to each meeting prepared.
  - Don't overcommit, and set boundaries.
- **Protégé:**
  - Own the agenda and know what you want.
  - Set realistic expectations and priorities.
  - Be open.
  - Don't expect the mentor to know everything.
  - Ask for feedback.
  - Come prepared and respect the mentor's time.
- The best mentors are often outside your own chain of command.

### Giving feedback (pp. 328-331)
- **Be specific and actionable.** "Your presentation wasn't effective" is useless. Instead, say what to change, for example: state the main message and the supporting topics up front, then cover each in order.
- **Seven steps:**
  1. **Prepare.** Use strategy and audience analysis, have the facts and unbiased appraisal data, and consider cultural factors, medium and timing.
  2. **Create a receptive setting.** Choose a neutral place or their office, or at least come out from behind your desk.
  3. **Be at ease and use "I" statements.** Make eye contact without challenging, and allow brief small talk. Instead of "You're not pulling your weight," try "I've noticed some distance from the team. Is there something I should know, or can I help?"
  4. **Set the context with facts.** For example, "this is your annual review," or a brief factual description of the problem.
  5. **Cover 3-4 points at most,** pausing after each for a response.
     - Focus on behavior, not personality.
     - Open with genuine positives, but never invent them.
     - Aim each criticism at something the person can correct.
  6. **Check understanding.** Invite questions.
  7. **Close with specific next steps and a timeline.**
- **GROW model** (Landsberg; Exhibit 12.5):
  - **Goal:** agree on the topic and the session's objective, and a longer-term aim if relevant.
  - **Reality:** invite self-assessment, give specific examples, check assumptions, and drop irrelevant history.
  - **Options:** cover the full range, invite the coachee's ideas first, offer yours carefully, and make sure choices are made.
  - **Wrap-up:** commit to actions, identify obstacles, make the steps specific and timed, and agree on support.

  Ask more than you tell, and act as a coach.
- **Receiving feedback:**
  - Listen without getting defensive and show interest.
  - If it's vague, ask for an example without sounding challenging: "Could you give me an example?" rather than "I have no idea what you mean."

### Networking (pp. 331-332)
- Networking is required for learning, recognition and advancement. Online networks help introverts, but they are public, so post carefully.
- **Before an event:**
  - Research the hosts and the likely attendees.
  - Practice a crisp one-minute introduction (an elevator speech): who you are, what you do, and your organization if people don't know it.
  - Prepare some small talk drawn from the news or professional activities.
  - If you get stuck, ask an open-ended question.
- **Networking plan (Application 12.4):**
  - Set goals framed around giving, not taking.
  - Track your efforts.
  - Map the decision makers and influencers.
  - List the relationships you need to build.
  - Choose societies to join and events to attend.
  - Improve your LinkedIn presence.

## Chapter 13: Transformational leadership through effective internal communication

People are motivated by respect, recognition and contribution more than by pay. Internal communication sets direction and builds motivation, and it is linked to performance. Employees need enough information and real chances to speak up, be heard and take part.

### Choose a leadership style (pp. 340-347)
Be authentic, but adapt your style to the situation (Fiedler's contingency research).

**Six styles (Goleman, Boyatzis and McKee; Exhibit 13.1):**

| Style | How it works | Effect on climate | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visionary | Moves people toward a shared dream | Most strongly positive | A new direction or vision is needed |
| Coaching | Links personal goals to the organization's | Highly positive | Building someone's long-term capability |
| Affiliative | Creates harmony and connection | Positive | Healing rifts, stressful times |
| Democratic | Gets input and commitment through participation | Positive | Building buy-in or getting input (slow in large organizations or in a crisis) |
| Pacesetting | Sets high, exciting goals | Often highly negative | Getting results from a team that is already motivated and competent |
| Commanding | Gives clear direction in an emergency | Often highly negative | Crisis, turnaround, problem employees (short-term only) |

**How each style communicates:**
- **Visionary:** highly visible, with frequent forums and social media.
- **Coaching:** a mentoring and training culture, with deep conversations about careers.
- **Affiliative:** walks the halls and stays accessible.
- **Democratic:** surveys people, collects input and listens. Its weakness is slowness at scale; DEC's decision-making became chaotic as the company grew.
- **Pacesetting:** decides with insiders and has little dialogue. It breeds a competitive, pressured climate; Enron is the cautionary case.
- **Commanding:** "because I say so." Welch used it for a turnaround, then shifted to a visionary style.

The four positive styles sound open ("we should work together to..."). Pacesetting and commanding sound closed ("it has been brought to my attention...").

**Transformational leaders** rely mostly on the visionary style. During change they draw on three things in particular:
1. **Authenticity and credibility.** Be transparent about objectives and methods, and model the behavior you expect.
2. **Emotional intelligence.** It reduces employees' resistance and cynicism about change.
3. **Mentoring and coaching.** Have a plan so that changes in behavior go deeper than the surface, and keep a leadership pipeline.

### How leaders shape culture (Schein, pp. 348-350)
- **Primary embedding mechanisms:**
  - what leaders pay attention to, measure and control
  - how they react to crises
  - how they allocate resources
  - deliberate role modeling, teaching and coaching
  - how they allocate rewards and status
  - how they recruit, promote and remove people
- **Secondary mechanisms:**
  - structure
  - systems and procedures
  - rites and rituals
  - physical space (a private executive floor and elevator say "hierarchy")
  - stories
  - formal creeds and charters
- To change a culture, change these mechanisms. Then both *tell* people about the change and *show* it, for example through new joint decision-making meetings.

### Five objectives of all employee communication
1. Educate people about the culture, vision and goals.
2. Motivate support.
3. Encourage discretionary effort.
4. Limit rumors and misunderstanding.
5. Align people behind the performance objectives.

Messages must be clear, consistent and targeted. Attend to both the *product* (the messages) and the *process* (the channels).

### Strategic Employee Communication Model (Exhibit 13.2, pp. 350-355)
The model ties the strategic objectives to the business processes. It has five best-practice components, and you can use them as benchmarks to audit a program:
1. **Supportive management.**
   - Every leader models the communication they expect and relays key messages to their direct reports in person.
   - Communication goes on performance reviews and is rewarded.
   - Leaders accept bad news without punishing the messenger. Fear of speaking up causes ethical lapses and wastes improvement ideas.
2. **Targeted messages.**
   - Keep the core meaning consistent inside and outside the organization, but adjust emphasis and wording for each group.
   - Business units can translate corporate messages into local, actionable versions, or even their own supporting vision. Enlist people close to each group to help with the wording.
3. **Effective media and forums.**
   - Don't assume a channel works; survey employees to find out.
   - Expensive newsletters often go unread.
   - Email can be overused; try the intranet, texts or microblogs.
   - If people prefer face to face, build it into routines (UPS's three-minute daily pre-shift meetings).
4. **Well-positioned staff.**
   - The senior communication officer needs a "seat at the table" at VP or president level.
   - Communication staff should be embedded across units so they can tailor messages and measure their impact, not just produce materials.
5. **Ongoing assessment.**
   - Measure individual communication in appraisals: do people share openly, invite input, and handle bad news well? This openness also deters unethical behavior.
   - Regularly survey a stratified sample to check whether key messages arrive and are understood. Then fix the breakdowns.

### Missions and visions (pp. 355-363)
- **Why they matter:** they inspire and guide behavior, ground planning, give people a shared identity, and keep everyone aligned during change. Collins and Porras found that "visionary" companies far outperformed the market.
- **Mission:** the reason the organization exists, in the "here and now." It is mainly internal and names the core products or services and a single noble purpose.
- **Vision:** an inspiring future reality achievable in a reasonable time frame. It usually begins "to become" or "to create." It doesn't matter much which statement is called which, as long as both exist and everyone knows which is which.
- **Test the mission:**
  - Is it inspiring and suggestive of excellence?
  - Does it make sense in the marketplace?
  - Is it stable but flexible?
  - Does it act as a beacon when everything else is changing?
  - Does it empower employees first and customers second?
- **Test the vision:**
  - Does it suggest goals and a direction?
  - Does it prepare for the future while honoring the past?
  - Is it specific and actionable for this company?
- **Three ways to build them (Jick):**
  - The CEO writes them. Suits start-ups and large hierarchical organizations.
  - The leader works with the senior team. Suits medium-sized, team-based organizations.
  - Employees build them bottom-up. Suits small, flat or nonprofit organizations; for example, 60 volunteers wrote Whole Foods' declaration.

  A hybrid usually works best.
- **Five-step build process:**
  1. **Draft.**
     - Mission questions: What do we do and why do we exist? What are our core and collateral offerings? Why does it matter? What would happen if we disappeared? What is our value proposition?
     - Vision questions: What does success take now, and in the future? What are our strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats? How can we add value for stakeholders? What do we want to become? How do we want competitors and the world to see us?
  2. **Clarify the meaning word by word.** Use an is/is-not table: for example, "market leader" means recognized by peers for innovation, not a specific market share.
  3. **Shorten it** to about 25 words or fewer. It should be memorable but not a mere slogan.
  4. **Add strategic objectives** that make the vision actionable. Link them with "by...". Make them measurable, both quantitative (financial, share, productivity) and qualitative (people development, reputation). Values or guiding principles can serve a similar role (Exhibits 13.3-13.5).
  5. **Test and refine in cascading, interactive meetings with employees**, level by level, then declare the statements final. This builds support and ownership, breaks down resistance, and improves the statements. Often the process unites people more than the finished statement does.

### Change communication (pp. 363-371)
- Most mergers miss their goals, usually because of clashes between cultures and people. Kotter names "undercommunication" as a main reason change efforts fail. Duck says leaders must be transparent about their own thinking if they want others to change.
- Leaders own the case for change, the implementation plan, and the explanation of what it means for the organization and for each person.
- **Scoping questions:**
  - Is this a major transformation or an incremental adjustment?
  - Does it affect the whole organization, or one group or process?
  - How many people does it affect?
  - Are the current internal channels and managers actually working? Fix them first if not.
- **Three levels of effort (Exhibit 13.6):**
  1. **Basic:** targeted, mostly one-way messages with no assessment. Fine for a policy change.
  2. **Strategic:** all media, mainly interactive meetings, with periodic assessment. For example, new performance measures.
  3. **Major change:** everything in level 2 plus workshops to redefine work habits and behavior, with frequent feedback. For example, a merger or a new direction.
- **Three-phase plan for major change (Exhibit 13.7).** Keep a high-level map, which reassures people that there is order amid the chaos, plus a detailed work plan. Update both through feedback loops.
  1. **Design the strategy and plan.**
     - Resist launching the change before deciding which specific behavior changes are needed. Use from/to analysis at every level.
     - Assess the current communication channels for breakdowns.
     - Set the audiences, media, messages, spokespersons and timing.
     - Form a dedicated **change communication team (CCT)**: multilevel, cross-functional and cross-cultural, including frontline staff; peers must trust them; they must be open and skilled facilitators; and they need time freed from their regular jobs.
     - The CCT assesses communication, builds the strategy, serves as ambassadors, and splits into subteams (strategy, vision, messages and materials, media and forums, assessment) with the CEO and senior team sponsoring it (Exhibit 13.8).
     - A frontline presence on the CCT makes the change less likely to be dismissed as another management fad.
  2. **Launch and make sure people understand.**
     - Start the message campaign through the chosen channels.
     - Move fast to stay ahead of the rumor mill.
     - Refresh the vision or create a new one, involving employees where you can.
     - Hold cascading, *interactive* meetings with all employees. Present the case for change and invite questions and reactions, so each person understands what it means for them and their job.
  3. **Monitor and adjust.**
     - Survey a cross-section of employees and run focus groups. Adjust the messages, media or number of meetings.
     - Hold **job redefinition sessions** led by direct supervisors, using from/to tables at the strategic level and then for each job (Exhibit 13.9). Making the change concrete reassures people, even when the change is large.
     - Involve supervisors throughout. Watch for hidden talent to promote or recruit as ambassadors.
     - Communicate successes along the way, and measure the results at the end.

## Chapter 14: Leadership through effective external relations

Reputation drives performance. Intangible assets may account for 60-70% of a company's value, and a well-publicized crisis can wipe out as much as 30% of its share value. Social media turns a local incident into a global one within hours. In the Domino's prank video, the company's response 48 hours later was too slow to prevent the damage, though it still helped. Organizations have an ethos just as people do, and leaders shape it. External relations covers advertising, promotion and marketing; this chapter focuses on PR: media, philanthropy, community, investor relations and publications.

### Seven-step external relations strategy (pp. 381-394)
1. **Clarify the purpose and strategic objectives.**
   - One central purpose from the top should govern all external activity.
   - Internal ethos and external image should match. A few core themes, drawn from the mission, vision and objectives, run through logos, websites, collateral and day-to-day messaging.
   - Rebrands follow the same process: research current perceptions (surveys of customers and non-customers), define the target messages, revise the mission, then test the new identity in focus groups.
2. **Identify and prioritize stakeholders.**
   - Possible stakeholders: media, community, customers, investors, analysts, board, partners, distributors, suppliers, trade associations, unions, interest groups, retirees, competitors, government agencies, and the general public.
   - To prioritize, ask:
     - Who shapes opinion?
     - Who is high-profile, respected or feared?
     - Who holds responsibility in the community?
     - Who is active?
     - Who belongs to an affected demographic?
     - Who has a financial or emotional stake?
     - Who has the most to gain or lose?
     - Who regulates you?
     - Who could undermine the campaign?
   - Plot stakeholders on a matrix of importance against difficulty of reaching them (Exhibit 14.4).
3. **Create, refine and test the messages.** External audiences are less motivated, less informed and less charitable than internal ones, so avoid jargon and don't assume goodwill. Messages must be:
   - **Honest.** Getting caught lying does more damage than telling an unpleasant truth.
   - **Clear.** Test them, for example with focus groups.
   - **Consistent.** Internal and external messages, and messages across audiences, must share the same core. Wording and emphasis can vary.
   - **Meaningful.** Don't water messages down into fluff or doublespeak to dodge bad news or legal risk. A euphemism-filled layoff notice ("rightsizing," "workforce adjustments") makes people feel deceived and destroys ethos.

   For broadcast, write your own 10-20 second sound bite that covers who, what, when, where, why and how. Keep the supporting detail ready.
4. **Select, limit and coach spokespersons.**
   - **The right rank:** too senior inflates the issue; too junior signals indifference. Firestone was criticized for sending a VP while Ford sent its CEO.
   - **Positive ethos and closeness to the facts:** for example, an on-site engineer explains causes, medical staff explain injuries, and a top executive expresses concern.
   - **Power sources to look for:** legitimate (title), referent (charisma) and expert (knowledge).
   - **Media training** is required before facing the press or launching any social media presence.
5. **Choose media and forums** (Exhibit 14.5). For a major event, use several at once; Putnam used letters, email, a press release, a press conference, web video and an advertisement.

| Medium | Strength | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Analyst briefing | Prepared statement plus Q&A | Needs solid financials and a credible, well-prepared spokesperson |
| Blog | Informal; invites feedback | Limited reach; unpredictable comments; needs constant oversight |
| Editorial | Puts your opinion on record in a credible outlet | Readers may see it as biased |
| Fact sheet or backgrounder | Bulleted highlights for press and analysts | Must be organized for skimming |
| Hotline | Direct questions from stakeholders | Responders need coaching for consistent answers |
| Microblog | Near real-time; easy to share | Brevity invites misunderstanding; immediacy invites carelessness |
| Podcast or online video | Cheap, quick, shareable; builds the brand | Damages the brand if poorly done; impact is hard to measure |
| Press conference | Prepared statements plus questions | Influential media may not come; requires rehearsal and anticipating questions |
| Media kit | Release, fact sheet, bios, photos, contacts, video | (none) |
| Press release | One page, most important information first (editors cut from the bottom), contacts included | Hit or miss unless followed up personally |
| RSS feed or social network | Reaches opted-in fans; builds the brand | Reaches subscribers only; damages the brand if neglected |
| Town hall | Controlled interaction with local stakeholders | Can turn into a gripe session if the news is bad; needs skilled facilitation |
| Advertising statement ("tombstone") | Exact control of wording | Expensive; may be dismissed as advertising |
| Website | Broad, fast and persuasive | Needs expert design, easy navigation, and contact information |

   **Press release format (Application 14.2):**
   - Answer who, what, where, when and why.
   - Put the key facts in the first sentence or two, with nice-to-knows at the end.
   - Put the contact name and number at the top.
   - Keep it to one page; if it runs over, mark "more" at the bottom of the first page.
   - Double-space with adequate margins.
   - Use concise, sound-bite sentences and proofread.
   - End with "-30-".
6. **Time it.**
   - **Unplanned news:** respond in real time.
   - **Planned announcements:** consider the context. Don't announce layoffs while promoting a lavish sponsorship. Avoid dates that undercut credibility; Google launched a product on April 1, and many people took it as a joke.
   - **Sequence carefully:** ideally employees hear first. Coordinate with markets and regulators; a merger, for example, may need to be announced before markets open on Monday.
   - **Have a plan ready** for handling negative stories.
7. **Monitor results.**
   - Watch published rankings (Fortune's Most Admired list, Harris RQ).
   - Use well-designed surveys and focus groups. Hire experts if you lack in-house skill.
   - Run continuous social listening on Twitter, blogs and activist sites, and search your own name.
   - Use clipping services.
   - Consider private online community panels that give fast, actionable feedback.
   - Plan routine monitoring plus periodic comprehensive assessments.

### Building reputation (pp. 394-395)
- The public's trust and feelings toward a company (ethos and pathos) dominate reputation measures:
  - outperforms the competition
  - admired and respected
  - trusted
  - plays a valuable social role
  - a good place to work
  - good feeling about the company
- A brand is what customers say it is, and they say it to each other online.
- **Fombrun's six levers:**
  1. Run campaigns that promote the company as a whole.
  2. Champion product quality and customer service.
  3. Screen employee activities for reputational side effects.
  4. Show environmental sensitivity.
  5. Invest in communication staff and PR firms.
  6. Practice good corporate citizenship.
- Present one unified voice across units. Every employee represents the company. One misstep in any area can do lasting harm.

### Working with the news media (pp. 395-398)
- **Why it matters:** earned media reaches more people more cheaply than advertising, and people trust "news" more than ads. Build relationships with reporters before you need them.
- **Before agreeing to talk, ask:**
  - What can we gain, and what can we lose?
  - Why would the media care about this?
  - Could we end up as part of a negative story about someone else?
  - Who is the reporter, and are they known for hard news or features?
  - Do we have all the facts?
  - Will we come across as credible?
  - Will this generate positive interest?
  - How could the interview turn negative?
- **Prepare:**
  - Build the strategy with a PR expert: context, audiences, objectives and key messages.
  - Know the content well enough to need minimal or no notes.
  - Research the reporter's background and interview style.
  - Agree on ground rules before the interview starts.
  - Rehearse in a setting like the real one.
  - Dress the part.
- **During the interview:**
  - Answer in short sound bites. If you talk at length, the reporter does the cutting and you lose control of the message.
  - Stay on your core message, even when the reporter steers elsewhere.
  - On the phone, keep notes handy but don't sound as if you are reading.
  - At a press conference, deliver the prepared statement, then answer questions from deep knowledge of the topic. Keep notes to brief facts and figures.
  - Stay composed and be yourself.
- **Ten rules (Burger; Exhibit 14.6):**
  1. Speak from the public's interest, not the company's.
  2. Use personal terms.
  3. Nothing is "off the record." If you don't want it quoted, don't say it.
  4. Lead with the most important fact.
  5. Never argue or lose your temper.
  6. Never repeat offensive or loaded words from a question, even to deny them.
  7. Answer a direct question directly.
  8. If you don't know, say "I don't know, but I'll find out."
  9. Tell the truth, even when it hurts.
  10. Don't exaggerate.
- **Afterward:** ask to review the story if the lead time allows. Thank the reporter. The relationship pays off in a crisis.

### Crisis communication (pp. 399-401)
- **Definition:** a crisis is an unexpected, non-routine event that creates high uncertainty and threatens the organization's priority goals. Relationships with stakeholders and the media built *before* a crisis make it easier to manage, but goodwill alone can't guarantee fair coverage.
- **Examples cited:**
  - Handled well: J&J's Tylenol response; US Airways' Hudson River landing.
  - Handled badly: Exxon Valdez (the CEO sent junior executives); BP's Gulf spill (minimized the disaster, mixed messages).
- **Eight guidelines:**
  1. **Have a crisis communication plan before you need it, and make sure everyone knows it.** No organization is immune.
  2. **Respond fast, because the first hours are critical.** Gather the facts quickly so you are first to the media and the public. Post updates on blogs and social media as information arrives, and point everyone to one central source, such as the website.
  3. **Put the right people forward with one consistent message.** A significant crisis requires the CEO. Train other executives too. At minimum, tell every employee where to refer media questions. Spokespersons should be visible and accessible.
  4. **See the crisis from the audience's point of view.** What do they need to know, and what worries them? Lead with facts, acknowledge feelings, and be honest and compassionate.
  5. **Use the web and social media as the virtual crisis center** for both internal and external audiences.
  6. **Review and update the plan regularly** as people and situations change, and tell the people responsible about changes. Keeping it on the intranet makes updates easy.
  7. **Monitor coverage** on blogs, Twitter, social networks and clipping services.
  8. **Hold a post-crisis review** to capture lessons, then set and quickly communicate the strategy for moving forward.
- **Leading out of the crisis:** the CEO defines the crisis (its size, severity and impact) and also explains how it will end: the new strategy, model and culture (Gerstner).
- **Reputation reflects reality.** You can't fix a reputation without changing the company. External messages must match what employees actually experience inside.

### Customer communication after a service failure (Applications 14.1-14.3)
The book covers this through cases rather than new guidance. The cases ask the reader to:
- write to every affected customer, with a concrete make-good offer and clear terms
- recommend ways to repair the damage on social media
- produce a multichannel media plan that adjusts the detail for each channel: website, blog and Twitter versus the press release
- write personal notices to clients explaining changes to the business

Their stated purpose: every external announcement both informs the public and builds an image.
